Chapter 07 — Karaikal Ammaiyaar

Thiruchitrambalam. Periya Puranam, Part 492. Thirunindra Sarukkam, Karaikal Ammaiyaar Puranam. Karaikal Ammaiyaar thinks that she must feed the Shiva devotee who has arrived. At that moment, only the sacred food (Thiruvamudhu) is ready. Sekkizhar Peruman continues to sing: "The good, fragrant mangoes, two of which her husband had kept for himself, she quickly took one of them, came, and served it. With a joyful heart, she fed the devotees, who were relieved of their suffering." Ammaiyaar, thinking that only rice was available, remembers that the two mangoes her husband had sent were in the house. Ammaiyaar becomes very happy. Sekkizhar Peruman refers to these mangoes as "good, fragrant mangoes." He uses two adjectives, "good" (nalla) and "fragrant" (naru), for the mangoes. He uses these two adjectives to describe the nature of how these fruits are used. "Good, fragrant mangoes." One is a "good" mango, and the other is a "fragrant" mango. The first "good" fruit is initially offered as a good feast to the devotee of Shiva. The other, the "fragrant" fruit, is exceedingly sweet and serves only to please the tongue. That is, the first mango was a good feast, a good mango, for the devotee of God, who is connected to the soul. The second mango, the "fragrant" mango, was used only for the taste of the tongue for the husband, who is connected to the body and its relatives. Therefore, with this meaning, Sekkizhar Peruman uses the two adjectives "good, fragrant mangoes." Ammaiyaar Punithavathiyar brings one of those two mangoes and offers it to the devotee. This "one" implies "unique" or "incomparable." This is because, firstly, this fruit goes to the devotee of Shiva. Through it going to the devotee of Shiva, it goes to God. After that, it becomes the cause for the manifestation of God's grace. It also serves as proof to reveal the truth to Ammaiyaar's husband. Thus, Ammaiyaar quickly brings one of the two mangoes and offers it to the devotee. Ammaiyaar is very happy. Ammaiyaar is delighted to see the devotee of Shiva relieve his hunger and eat to his heart's content. From birth and learning to speak, Ammaiyaar has cultivated a deep love and devotion to the sacred feet of Shiva. This is the place where God will relieve all suffering and troubles. "Due to the weakness of old age and the intense, burning hunger, the tired devotee, who had arrived, joyfully ate the soft, delicious food with the mangoes. The women with soft, flower-like hair were pleased and left." This is the next verse. Thus, when Ammaiyaar offered the sacred food and mango, the devotee, who had arrived very tired due to advanced age, the resulting weakness, and intense hunger, ate the delicious food offered by Ammaiyaar along with the mangoes. The reason for the devotee's extreme fatigue was his old age, weakness, and the intensity of his hunger. However, Sekkizhar Peruman honors the devotee who came in this state by calling him "Thiruthondar" (holy devotee). Therefore, the devotee who came was not truly exhausted in this manner. The word "Thiruthondar" implies that he came to illustrate the greatness of Ammaiyaar's love. Thus, the devotee, having eaten the sacred food and mango offered by Ammaiyaar to his heart's content, leaves the place very happily.

Sekkizhar Peruman continues to sing: "After he left, the merchant, her husband, arrived at his grand house in the middle of the great day. Having bathed beautifully, he entered, and his chaste wife, who prepared the food, served him dutifully." Thus, after the devotee left, Paramadattan, Ammaiyaar's husband, arrived home in the middle of that good day. The time he arrived was noon, around 15 nazhigai (a measure of time) into the day. This time was considered suitable for eating by our ancestors. In those days, there was no custom of eating small meals in between, like snacks before lunch. These are all modern habits. Eating small meals between two large meals leads to illness. We should remember the ancient saying, "Once a yogi, twice a bhogi, thrice a rogi" (one who eats once is a yogi, twice a pleasure-seeker, thrice a sick person). Similarly, the custom of combining breakfast with lunch is also a recent habit. It is also detrimental to our health. Thus, Paramadattan comes to his house for lunch. Sekkizhar Peruman refers to it as "the grand house." This house becomes elevated because a devotee had previously partaken of sacred food there. Now, it is the house where divine grace is about to manifest, so it becomes an elevated house, a high house. Paramadattan, having arrived, bathes and completes his rituals. He has come from his place of work. Therefore, it is customary to eat after bathing and completing rituals. Accordingly, he bathes, performs his rituals, and sits down to eat. "Having bathed beautifully, he entered, and his chaste wife, who prepared the food, served him dutifully." Serving food to one's husband is an essential duty of a wife in married life. This is a duty of chastity that must be upheld without fail every day. Ammaiyaar's previous offering of hospitality was related to her right to perform sacred service (Thiruthondu) stemming from her devotion to Shiva. Now, feeding her husband is one of the duties of a chaste wife. This is a duty performed for the husband, who is a bodily relative in this birth. Thus, Ammaiyaar arranges the food for her husband, placing the food, curries, and other items in the proper manner. Along with that, Ammaiyaar brings the one remaining mango from the two her husband had sent earlier (after giving one to the devotee) and places it on her husband's plate. Here, Sekkizhar Peruman states that Ammaiyaar served "sweet food with curries." While referring to vegetables as "curries" without any adjective, Sekkizhar Peruman, when serving the sacred food (rice) to her husband, refers to it as "sweet food." This is because this food (rice) was what remained after the Shiva devotee had partaken of it. Therefore, it is highly special. Thus, it becomes sweet food. The vegetables and curries that were there could not be offered to the devotee earlier. Therefore, Sekkizhar Peruman refers to them simply as "curries" without any adjective. Thus, she serves everything exquisitely on the plate, exactly as it should be served. She places each food item appropriately where it should be. At that moment, Punithavathiyar offers the one remaining mango from the two. Paramadattan, who eats it, finds the mango very delicious.

He thinks about it and is delighted. Therefore, his desire for that mango increases greatly. The reason this mango is so delicious is that it was offered by his wife. This is food given with love. This mango was already delicious. Moreover, Ammaiyaar offers it with great love. Therefore, it tastes even more delicious. Even if food is not inherently delicious, food offered with love will taste delicious. Even if food is delicious, if it is not offered with love, that deliciousness may seem diminished. Here, this mango is already very delicious. Additionally, it is a mango offered by Ammaiyaar with great love. Therefore, it tastes even more delicious. Thus, Paramadattan, who ate that sweet and delicious mango, was captivated by its taste. Therefore, after eating one of the two mangoes, Paramadattan asks Ammaiyaar to bring the second mango as well. We will continue to reflect. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 08 — Karaikal Ammaiyaar

Thiruchitrambalam. Periya Puranam, Part 493. Thirunindra Sarukkam, Karaikal Ammaiyaar Puranam. Paramadattan ate the mango offered by Ammaiyaar, and its taste greatly captivated him. Paramadattan became enslaved to that taste. Therefore, remembering that he had sent two mangoes, he ate one and, finding it very delicious, asked Ammaiyaar to bring the other fruit. At this point, Paramadattan becomes enslaved to the pleasure of his tongue, to the taste sensation of his tongue. By becoming enslaved to the taste of his tongue, he loses all other good and righteous qualities. Sivakavimani Aiya lists the good qualities Paramadattan loses by becoming enslaved to the taste of his tongue. Firstly, he had sent two fruits. Ammaiyaar offered only one for him to eat. Therefore, before asking for the second mango, he should have first asked if the second fruit was still available. Paramadattan did not ask that. He should have first asked if the fruit was there or if it had already been used. Then, after confirming that the fruit was there, he could have asked for it. What prevented him from asking this way was the result of Paramadattan being enslaved to the taste of the mango. Secondly, even if one of the two mangoes had remained unused, it is the duty of a good husband to wish that his wife also eat that fruit and enjoy it, just as he enjoyed tasting it. If there were two mangoes, he should have thought that if one was for him, the second was for his wife. What prevented him from thinking this way was also the result of his enslavement to the taste of his tongue. It is a husband's duty to consider husband and wife as equal partners in good and bad, and in possessions. These are the good qualities Paramadattan lost in ordinary worldly life by being enslaved to the taste of his tongue. Now, even at a higher level, as the saying goes, "May the joy I received spread throughout the world," it is one's duty to wish that others also experience joy as one has. Sharing food with the four categories of ancestors (thenpulathar) and the three categories of renunciates (thuranthar), seeing them as oneself, is an important duty and righteousness of a householder. Paramadattan loses this quality too. Thus, by being enslaved to the taste of his tongue, Paramadattan not only lost these good qualities but also, by asking Ammaiyaar to bring another fruit, and through the subsequent events, he is about to lose Ammaiyaar, who was a good wife, from his life. Paramadattan is about to lose Ammaiyaar, who is a divine being capable of guiding him on the right path and elevating him to a higher path, by being enslaved to the taste of his tongue. These are the lessons we must learn from this story. Even in these days, by being enslaved to the taste of the tongue and to the sensory pleasure of taste, this modern world is losing many good qualities and acquiring many evils by becoming addicted to various feasts, snacks, large meals, and beverages. Therefore, Sivakavimani Aiya writes this text so that the world may realize these truths and be saved. Now, when Paramadattan asked for another mango, Ammaiyaar left the place as if to fetch the fruit. Ammaiyaar had offered the first fruit to the Shiva devotee. Without telling Paramadattan about this at that moment, she leaves the place as if to fetch the second fruit. She goes inside the house.

Because some thoughts are running through Ammaiyaar's mind. Her husband asks for a mango. She fears that her husband might think it was wrong of her to use the mangoes he sent, not keeping them for him as he instructed. In the worldly sense, chastity is defined as not deviating from one's word. Her husband might consider this a blemish on her chastity in the eyes of the world. He might also think that she acted contrary to the promise she made at the wedding, "As long as I live, I will dedicate my body and soul to you." Ammaiyaar went inside, thinking all these thoughts, as if to fetch the fruit. But did Ammaiyaar think that offering the fruit to the Shiva devotee was wrong? Certainly not. Ammaiyaar never thought that offering the mango to the Shiva devotee was wrong. This is because she, her husband, all their possessions, and even the mangoes that were currently their possessions, all belonged to Lord Shiva. As the saying goes, "Everything is your servant, everything is your possession," everything is Shiva's servant and possession. All conscious beings are servants of God, and all inanimate objects are possessions of God. Therefore, offering the mangoes, which are Shiva's possessions, to the devotees who have attained Shiva and become his servants, is a very appropriate and correct action. Furthermore, the benefit of acquiring possessions, the benefit of acquiring wealth, is realized when we dedicate it to devotees. That is the true benefit. Moreover, the joy that comes to Shiva's devotees from eating the mangoes is a much more lasting joy and results in Shiva's merit, compared to the joy Ammaiyaar and her husband would experience from eating them. Therefore, she has done good for her husband. It is concluded that Ammaiyaar acted without the slightest blemish to the path of chastity. Our Ammaiyaar knows all this well. Therefore, Ammaiyaar never thought that offering the mango to the Shiva devotee was wrong. However, her husband did not have the maturity to analyze and understand all this. Even if she told him, he did not have the maturity to accept it at that moment. Therefore, thinking that his mind should not change, the great lady moved away from there and went inside. In reality, Ammaiyaar did not go inside to bring the fruit. But her going inside appeared as if she was going to bring the fruit. This is because it is not graceful for a good wife to say "no" to something her husband has desired. But she also did not have the courage to bring that fruit. She had to give the fruit her husband asked for, but it was not there. Therefore, Ammaiyaar goes inside with the mindset that "let what is to happen, happen according to God's will, as God has ordained." Thus, the idea of trying to bring the fruit is evident here. But the fruit is not there. Therefore, Ammaiyaar goes inside with the mindset that "let it happen according to the Lord's will." Sekkizhar Peruman continues to sing: "Standing there, she was troubled, what could she do for the rare fruit? Forgetting herself, she thought of the feet of the Lord who comes to help in times of distress, and by His grace, a very sweet fruit appeared in her hand." This is the 25th verse in Ammaiyaar Puranam. This verse describes the manifestation of God's grace. Ammaiyaar, who went inside as if to bring the fruit, stands there and feels disheartened. What else could Ammaiyaar do to obtain that other fruit? Forgetting herself, she remembers God.

She remembers the sacred feet of Lord Shiva, who comes to help in times of distress, the Lord who rides the bull (Rishabaroodar), placing them in her heart and perceiving them with the awareness of divine grace. At that moment, by the grace of the Lord, a very sweet mango appeared in Ammaiyaar's hands. Ammaiyaar goes inside with great sorrow, unable to say that there is no fruit and unable to bring the fruit. Ammaiyaar thinks that it is better to bring the fruit and give it than to cause sorrow to her husband by saying that there is no fruit. Ammaiyaar thinks, what is the way if she needs that fruit? As the saying goes, "Those who forget themselves and remember you are in a pure state of mind," she completely forgets herself and remembers only Lord Shiva. She remembers the feet of the Lord who comes to help in times of distress. She remembers those sacred feet to which her love has been attached ever since she was born and learned to speak. She deeply experiences and understands, her mind completely absorbed in the sacred feet. At that moment, a very sweet fruit appeared in Ammaiyaar's hands. That fruit came into Ammaiyaar's hands. The fruit that came was there. This fruit was sweeter than the sweet fruit the merchant had eaten. Therefore, Sekkizhar Peruman refers to it as "athimadhura kani" (exceedingly sweet fruit). "Athimadhuram" is a type of medicinal substance. It is a substance that quenches thirst if there is too much sweetness. Therefore, the fruit that came there was not a mango, but an "athimadhura kani," used by Sekkizhar Peruman in this sense. The implication here is that after eating this, the merchant's worldly thirst for domestic life will be quenched. The sweet fruit the merchant ate earlier gave him a pleasant taste and created more and more desire for that taste. But this "athimadhura kani" that came now, after eating it, did not give the merchant sensory pleasure from the sweet fruit. Instead, he gained an awareness of Shiva's welfare and the honor of worshipping Ammaiyaar. Therefore, this becomes an incomparable fruit. Sekkizhar Peruman refers to it as "athimadhura kani ondru" (one exceedingly sweet fruit) that appeared in her hand. The word "ondru" (one) here signifies not just a single mango in number, but also an incomparable mango. In Tamil, "ondru" can also mean "incomparable." Thus, Sekkizhar Peruman beautifully sings about the event of God's divine grace. We will continue to reflect. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 09 — Karaikal Ammaiyaar

Thiruchitrambalam. Periya Puranam, Part 494. Thirunindra Sarukkam, Karaikal Ammaiyaar Puranam. By the grace of God, an exceedingly sweet fruit falls into Ammaiyaar's hands. Sekkizhar Peruman then composes the next verse: "When she brought it and joyfully placed it, its taste was superior to ambrosia. He said, 'This is not the mango you gave me before; it is rare to obtain in the three worlds. Where else did you get it?' and asked the woman with the beautiful bangles." Ammaiyaar brings the mango that appeared in her hands and places it on Paramadattan's plate with great joy. Paramadattan had asked for the second mango, thinking it was the second of the two mangoes he had sent. But what Ammaiyaar brought and placed was not the second mango he had in mind. This was a mango that came by the grace of God. That is why Sekkizhar Peruman begins this verse with "matru adhanai" (that other one). This fruit is different from the fruit her husband thought of, so he refers to it as "matru." Ammaiyaar places and serves this mango on Paramadattan's plate with great joy. This is because she was greatly troubled about what to do about the second mango. That trouble and weakness now disappear, and she experiences joy. This is because this fruit, which came by God's grace, helped Ammaiyaar's worldly life and spiritual life to proceed without deviation. Therefore, with that joy, she serves the fruit on Paramadattan's plate. As soon as the fruit is placed, Paramadattan eats it with great desire. This is because he had already been enslaved to the taste of the first fruit he ate and had unrighteously asked for the second fruit with great desire. Therefore, as soon as the fruit is placed, he immediately takes and eats it. Only after eating does he realize that this second fruit has a completely different and unique taste from the first fruit. Therefore, he clearly identifies it. He realizes that this second mango is superior to ambrosia. He realizes that this second mango is superior to ambrosia in taste and benefit. In taste, it is a taste superior to ambrosia. The ambrosia of the gods only helps to prolong life. But this mango is a mango that came by the grace of Lord Shiva. Therefore, it helps to reveal Shiva's grace. Therefore, this is a mango superior to ambrosia. Furthermore, this mango made Paramadattan realize Ammaiyaar for who she truly was, that she was not an ordinary human being, but a great divine being. Therefore, this is a mango superior to ambrosia. Moreover, how did this mango come to Paramadattan? One of the two mangoes belonging to Paramadattan was offered as sacred food to the devotee of Shiva by Ammaiyaar. Because Paramadattan's mango was offered to the devotee of Shiva, this second mango returns by Shiva's grace, as if with interest. Paramadattan tastes and eats it with great delight. Avvai Piratti, in her work "Moodurai," sang in the first verse: "When good is done to someone, do not ask when that good will return. The steadfast, growing coconut tree, having drunk water through its roots, gives it back through its head." If a good person helps someone, they should not wonder when or how that help will return. There is no rule that the benefit of that help must return in the same way it was given. The benefit of that returned help can reach him in any other form, according to the rule laid down by God. How? We plant a coconut sapling, water its roots, and grow the coconut tree. The coconut tree, having taken water through its roots, gives tender coconut water from its top, which is its head. The coconut tree took water from its roots, so it could have given tender coconut water through its roots, but it is not like that. It takes water through its roots and gives tender coconut water through its head. Therefore, it is not that the help we give will return to us as merit in the same place and in the same way. The benefit of that merit will be added to us by the grace of God in some other way. Therefore, here, one of the two mangoes belonging to Paramadattan was used as sacred food for the devotee of Shiva. Therefore, this merit, by the grace of God, now returns to Paramadattan as a mango with a taste far superior to the first mango and yielding far greater benefits. Paramadattan is a merchant. Therefore, just as a loan returns with interest, that ordinary mango belonging to Paramadattan became sacred food for the devotee of Shiva through Ammaiyaar, and now, by the grace of Lord Shiva, the fruit of that merit is arranged for Paramadattan to taste a mango that came by Shiva's grace. The subtlety of this is very profound. Paramadattan asks Ammaiyaar, "This mango is not one of the two mangoes I sent. This mango is a mango that cannot be found in these three worlds. So how did you get it?" Paramadattan's statement that this mango is not found in the three worlds unknowingly contains a truth. This mango is a mango not found in the three worlds. This mango is not one of the mangoes that are products of maya, which grow in these worlds subject to the illusory realms of the upper world, middle world, and lower world. This is a fruit that is the result of Shiva's realm, which is beyond all realms. This truth comes out of Paramadattan's mouth unknowingly. Therefore, this mango is very rare to obtain in the three worlds. Therefore, he asks Ammaiyaar, "Where else did you get it, if not from these three worlds?" But Ammaiyaar took the question to mean, "Who gave this? This is not what I received. This is not the mango I gave. From whom else did you get this mango?" This question was completely unexpected for Ammaiyaar. Ammaiyaar never even imagined that Paramadattan would ask, "How did you get this mango? Who gave you this mango?" Suddenly, Paramadattan asks this question because of the unique taste of the mango. Upon hearing this, Ammaiyaar trembles. She shivers. Sekkizhar Peruman composes the verse as follows: "Hearing those words, the woman, full of grace, did not say that it was not proper to reveal the great grace bestowed by the Lord with the glorious matted hair. But she could not avoid saying that it was not right to go against her husband's words, following the path of chastity. She trembled." Thus, Sekkizhar Peruman illustrates Ammaiyaar's mental and physical state there. The question Paramadattan asked makes Ammaiyaar tremble. This is because

Because Ammaiyaar has two dilemmas here. Ammaiyaar cannot tell the truth of what happened, nor can she remain silent. This is because this second mango is a mango that came by the grace of God. Generally, it is a rule, a tradition, that one should not reveal the act of Shiva's grace that has happened to them. Therefore, because of this tradition, she should not tell her husband how this mango came, as it is an act of grace performed by Shiva for her. So she remains silent. But at the same time, she cannot remain silent. This is because a chaste wife should not hide any truth from her husband. Moreover, she must act according to her husband's words. Therefore, she must answer the question her husband has asked. At the same time, this mango was manifested by the grace of God. Therefore, she must also remain silent about the tradition of not sharing that act of grace with others. Thus, Ammaiyaar is in a dilemma in two ways. These conflicting thoughts occur in her mind. As a result, Ammaiyaar's body trembles. Earlier, when Paramadattan asked for the second mango, she was troubled and disheartened. But now, Ammaiyaar's state is one of even greater mental distress and confusion. Therefore, it does not remain at the mental level but manifests as trembling in Ammaiyaar's body. With all these conflicting thoughts, Ammaiyaar decides that it is best to tell the truth as it is, whatever it may be. "By the virtue of saying what happened, she bowed in her heart to the sacred feet of the Lord with the dark-throated neck. When her husband asked, 'Who gave this fruit that came?' the woman with the fragrant, flowing hair told him exactly what had happened." This is the next verse composed by Sekkizhar Peruman. Whatever the case, Ammaiyaar adopts the high virtue, the high quality, of speaking the truth as it is. As stated in the previous verse, she overcomes the mental confusion and, with courage, decides that whatever happens, it is her duty to tell the truth of what happened. If so, is Ammaiyaar violating the rule that one should not reveal the great act of grace God has performed for them? It is not like that. Here, Ammaiyaar is going to tell the truth. God is truth and the embodiment of truth. Therefore, speaking the truth as it is does not go against the grace of Lord Shiva. Therefore, Ammaiyaar is not violating that rule. Moreover, the fault of not acting according to her husband's words does not fall upon Ammaiyaar. That is why Sekkizhar Peruman refers to this as good conduct, virtue. Having decided this, Ammaiyaar bows to the sacred feet of Lord Shiva, who has the dark-throated neck (Maithazhaiyum Kandar). This is because the event Ammaiyaar is about to describe is the grace of God. She is now going to reveal that grace, which should not be revealed to others. Therefore, before that, she bows to the sacred feet of God. Here, when referring to the Lord, Sekkizhar Peruman sings about the Lord with the dark-throated neck (Thiruneelakandam). God transformed that poison into ambrosia and, moreover, made it gracious and kept it in His own neck forever. That Thiruneelakandam has the power to remove the suffering of those who remember it and bestow joy. We can recall here the story of Thiruneelakanda Nayanar and the meaning of the Thiruneelakandam Pathikam composed by Thirugnanasambandar Peruman. Therefore, bowing to the feet of the Lord of Thiruneelakandam, Ammaiyaar tells Paramadattan what truly happened. Ammaiyaar tells Paramadattan all the events, from the Shiva devotee coming to eat sacred food to the mango now appearing by the grace of God. Ammaiyaar tells Paramadattan that it was the grace of Lord Shiva that gave this mango. We will continue to reflect. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 10 — Karaikal Ammaiyaar

Thiruchitrambalam. Periya Puranam, Part 495. Thirunindra Sarukkam, Karaikal Ammaiyaar Puranam. Ammaiyaar tells Paramadattan that the mango came by the grace of God. However, Paramadattan does not understand it. He remains unconvinced as to how a mango could be obtained by God's grace. "Hearing 'God's grace,' the householder did not understand. He looked at the woman with fragrant flower-like hair and said, 'If this is truly the grace of the Lord with the glorious matted hair, then bring another flawless fruit by His grace.' " This is the next verse. Ammaiyaar answers her husband, who asked where she got this fruit, which has a taste not found in these three worlds, and who gave it, by saying that it was the grace of God. Hearing this, Paramadattan, the owner of the house, becomes confused. He does not become clear. Paramadattan is a man who lives in ordinary worldly life. He is not very familiar or practiced in the nature of Shiva's grace. Paramadattan is devoid of even a little devotion and quality of servitude to Lord Shiva. Therefore, without understanding this clearly, he tells Ammaiyaar, "If a fruit came to you by God's grace, then bring another fruit by God's grace again." Paramadattan does not have faith in divine grace. That is why he asks, "If this is truly God's grace, then bring another fruit." Sekkizhar Peruman specifically indicates that he is a person who lacks faith in God's grace. If someone is devoted to God, Sekkizhar Peruman will praise them in various ways as one who has taken true servitude to the Lord. At the same time, if Sekkizhar Peruman needs to refer to an ordinary person like Paramadattan, he will not explicitly state their shortcomings but will subtly imply them so that we can understand them ourselves. Therefore, it is the tradition of great people to subtly imply the shortcomings of others without explicitly stating them, and to clearly explain and elaborate on the good qualities and excellences of others. Sekkizhar Peruman shows us this here. Thus, Ammaiyaar hears what her husband says. What does she do upon hearing it? Sekkizhar Peruman continues to sing: "Moving away, the wife, praising the Lord who wears snakes as ornaments, said, 'If you do not grant this here, my words will be false.' By grace, a mango came, and when she placed it in his hand, he took it with wonder." Ammaiyaar, hearing what her husband said, again goes from that place to another part of the house. There, she worships Lord Shiva, who wears snakes as ornaments. Ammaiyaar is Paramadattan's wife. Therefore, as soon as she hears her husband's words, she goes inside due to her chastity, which dictates that she must act according to her husband's words. Going inside, she again worships Lord Shiva. Sekkizhar Peruman refers to this as "praising the Lord who wears snakes as ornaments." The meaning is that she worships Lord Shiva, who wears snakes as ornaments. We have already seen that Ammaiyaar, in her "Arputha Thiruvanthathi," sang many verses about God wearing snakes as ornaments. Similarly, for that reason, in Ammaiyaar's Puranam, Sekkizhar Peruman, when praising Lord Shiva in many places, praises him as one who wears snakes as ornaments. Lord Shiva is one who wears poisonous, evil snakes, transforming them into ambrosia. Similarly, the implication here is that Lord Shiva will purify souls with defects and make them sit by His side. Therefore, great people always remember their shortcomings, pray, and ask God to remove those shortcomings and take them into His fold.

It is the tradition of great people to pray to God for what they need. Therefore, Ammaiyaar comes here and stands before the Lord, praying. She stands praying to Lord Shiva for another fruit. Even if Lord Shiva gives the fruit now or not, His grace will not be false. Ammaiyaar is firmly aware that Lord Shiva's grace is eternal. However, if He does not give the fruit, all the events Ammaiyaar told her husband will become false. Therefore, Ammaiyaar's words must not be false. If Ammaiyaar's words become false, it will be a blemish on her devotion to the Lord. Therefore, so that her devotion to the Lord is never blamed, Ammaiyaar stands praying to the Lord for another mango. As she prayed, a mango came into Ammaiyaar's hands by the grace of Lord Shiva. The mango given by divine grace earlier was for Paramadattan to taste. But this mango, given now, was given as a witness to divine grace. It was not given for Paramadattan to eat. He doubted divine grace and did not believe it. Therefore, this mango came to prove the truth of divine grace and to show a witness to the Lord's grace. As this mango appeared in Ammaiyaar's hands, she takes it and gives it to Paramadattan. Paramadattan, greatly astonished, takes the fruit, wondering what a miracle it is. Earlier, Ammaiyaar served the mango that came by divine grace on Paramadattan's plate for him to eat. That was for his hunger and taste. But now he asks for it as a witness to divine grace. Therefore, Ammaiyaar gives it into her husband's hands instead of serving it on a plate. As soon as Paramadattan takes the mango in his hands, the mango disappears from his hands. Paramadattan, seeing the fruit he had taken disappear, becomes afraid. What fear did he have? He feared that it was improper to live with Ammaiyaar anymore. He feared that he was not worthy to live with Ammaiyaar. He realizes that Ammaiyaar is a divine being, a goddess. Therefore, he decides that it is no longer possible to live with Ammaiyaar, a divine being. He thinks that he is not worthy to live with her and decides to leave Ammaiyaar. Should he leave Ammaiyaar? Can he just abandon Ammaiyaar? Paramadattan is currently living in Karaikal. He lives by doing business in the house provided by Ammaiyaar's father on Ammaiyaar's behalf. Therefore, in this situation, he cannot remove Ammaiyaar from his life. So he decides that he must leave that place. But he was afraid to tell anyone else about all these events. Therefore, without telling anyone else about this, he kept it to himself and decided that he must somehow leave Ammaiyaar. Having decided this, he waited for the right moment. Until then, even though he lived in the same house with Ammaiyaar, he did not live with her. Therefore, to leave Ammaiyaar, he tells his relatives that he needs to go to sea and do business in foreign countries. In those days, it was a very common practice for men to leave their wives to earn money. Therefore, even when Paramadattan said this, no questions arose from his relatives or others. He is a businessman, a merchant. So his relatives all think that he intends to go to many countries across the sea to do business. Therefore, they arrange for the necessary ship. They build a ship and hire skilled people like sailors and those proficient in operating ships. Paramadattan loads the ship with all the goods that are very rare to find in the countries he intended to visit for business. Thus, having loaded the ship with goods, on an auspicious day, Paramadattan worshipped Varuna, the sea god, and set sail. "Seek wealth even by crossing the seas" is an ancient saying by Avvai Moothati. Therefore, in those days, doing business by sea and earning money was a common practice among Tamils from ancient times. In ancient Tamil texts like Pattinappalai and Silappathikaram, it is found how Tamils sailed ships to other countries, did business, and prospered greatly. We can see all those details. Moreover, it is customary for one who goes to earn money to go alone. It is not customary to take one's wife along. Therefore, for this reason, Paramadattan leaves Ammaiyaar in Karaikal and sets sail with the ship. Even in those days, Tamils were skilled in shipbuilding, ship operation, and cross-sea trade, which is evident from this. In that period, merchants prospered by taking our country's goods across the sea to foreign countries and selling them there, and by bringing goods that were very rare in Tamil Nadu from foreign countries and selling them here. This practice continued in Tamil Nadu until the time of our Sekkizhar Peruman. Thus, Paramadattan crosses the sea. He goes to all the countries he intended to do business in, conducts business very well, acquires many goods there, and then boards the ship again and arrives at a coastal town in the Pandya country. This town is mentioned as "Kanakalam" in the Sanskrit Brahmanda Purana. We can infer that it is near the confluence of the Thamirabarani River with the sea. Sekkizhar Peruman does not specify which of the many port cities in the Pandya country this town is. Sekkizhar Peruman refers to it as "a town in the country of spreading waters" in his verse. "Padarpunal" can be rephrased as "punal padar" (spreading waters), meaning a place where rivers flow and meet the sea. Thus, we can understand it as a town located where the Thamirabarani River meets the sea. There were many such coastal towns in the Pandya country. Thirumandira Nagar, now called Thoothukudi, Sethukkarai, Kulasekharan Pattinam, etc., are all port cities in the Pandya country. It is an assumption that this town might have been one of them. Thus, Paramadattan, having arrived at a port town in the Pandya country, starts doing business there with all the goods he had acquired. He also married the daughter of a good merchant there. He lives again, having remarried in a port town in the Pandya country, leaving Punithavathiyar Ammaiyaar in Karaikal. But he lives ensuring that no one knows that he was already married and had deceived Punithavathiyar Ammaiyaar. In all other aspects, he lives a very honest and good life, but he hides the deception he committed against Ammaiyaar from everyone else and lives very happily with the woman he married a second time. Therefore, he was already married, and the Ammaiyaar he married was a divine being. Fearing the divinity of that divine woman, he could not live with her, so he came to a foreign country to do business, told that lie, separated from Ammaiyaar, then did business, came to the Pandya country, lived there, hid all this, and then remarried and lived this way. Thus, the merchant Paramadattan, who did very well in business in the Pandya port, had a daughter born to him as a result of his domestic life with his second wife. Sekkizhar Peruman sings this as: "He obtained a girl, like a lamp of increasing light, with great difficulty." He was delighted to name that girl Punithavathi, Ammaiyaar's sacred name. A girl, like a lamp of increasing light, came to Paramadattan. That girl came to Paramadattan. He first named that girl Punithavathi, Ammaiyaar's sacred name. This event is the original cause for the Saivite devotees to joyfully name their children Punithavathiyar, or Punithavathi, after Ammaiyaar. That is why Sekkizhar Peruman, who sings about this event, sings that "a girl, like a lamp of increasing light, came and arrived." Generally, every girl child is born to illuminate and make their lineage prosper. Especially if the first child is a girl, it is a worldly custom that the lineage will grow and prosper. Therefore, for these two reasons, Sekkizhar Peruman praises the girl child named Punithavathi as "a girl, like a lamp of increasing light." We will continue to reflect. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 11 — Karaikal Ammaiyaar

Thiruchitrambalam. Periya Puranam, Part 496. Thirunindra Sarukkam, Karaikal Ammaiyaar Puranam. Paramadattan performed the auspicious naming ceremony for his daughter, giving her the sacred name of Punithavathiyar Ammaiyaar. Generally, for girls, marriage is considered the first ceremony. However, considering his wealth, he decided to perform naming and other ceremonies for his daughter. Thus, on an auspicious day, he named his daughter Punithavathi, Ammaiyaar's sacred name. This is because Paramadattan knew that Ammaiyaar was a divine being. Therefore, he thought that he was not worthy to live with Ammaiyaar and left her. So, thinking that his connection with Ammaiyaar should not continue, he performs this ceremony, praying, "I am performing a ceremony by naming my child after you. From now on, I wish to be without the connection of you as my wife." Just as people offer Pongal and worship the presiding deities of snakes and other creatures, praying, "May you not appear before our eyes anymore, may poisonous creatures like snakes not appear before our eyes," Paramadattan, following this minor deity worship method, prays to Ammaiyaar, "I wish to be without the connection of you as my wife," and names his daughter after Ammaiyaar. While this merchant was living there, at the same time in Karaikal, our Ammaiyaar continued to perform the dharmas of "pasu dharma" (duties towards creatures) and "pathi dharma" (duties towards husband). While living in household life, one must perform the worldly "pasu dharmas" and also the "pathi dharmas." Ammaiyaar had been performing both of these properly. This period must have been at least two years. In such circumstances, a close relative of Ammaiyaar hears that Ammaiyaar's husband is living in a port town in the Pandya country. Hearing this, and wishing to confirm it, he sends a close relative to investigate and report on the truth of this news. The relative who was sent goes there and confirms that Paramadattan is doing very well in business and is living with a second wife and a daughter. He then returns and informs Ammaiyaar's relative. Upon hearing this, Ammaiyaar's relatives are distressed. They suffer great sorrow. In this situation, the relatives decide to take Ammaiyaar back to her husband. In reality, Paramadattan should have been with Ammaiyaar; that is the norm. But instead, he is staying in another town and living with another marriage. This is truly a punishable offense. Leaving Punithavathi Ammaiyaar deceitfully and marrying again and living that way means that the merchant is guilty of deviating from his duty. Therefore, they could fight him, file a lawsuit, and even get royal punishment according to the law for this crime. These are actions that ordinary people would take. But Ammaiyaar's relatives are highly moral and have high ideals. Therefore, they decide that the wife should be where the husband is, and the wife should be with the husband; that is the custom of their lineage. Therefore, Ammaiyaar's relatives decide to take her to Paramadattan and begin their journey. They arrange this journey with great sorrow. This is because Ammaiyaar is very young. Therefore, they decide that she should not be separated from her husband. They arrange for a palanquin. They seat Ammaiyaar in the palanquin, and the relatives travel from Karaikal to that town in the Pandya country. "In the great palanquin, they seated the graceful, peacock-like woman, like the unique Thirumagal (Lakshmi) residing on a lotus seat. Surrounded by loving relatives, both men and women, and the sweet-voiced woman herself, they traveled a long distance." This is the verse composed by Sekkizhar Peruman. They carry the beautiful, peacock-like Punithavathi Ammaiyaar in the large, beautiful palanquin. They seat Punithavathi Ammaiyaar, like the unique Ambikai (Parvati) residing on a lotus seat, and arrange a curtain for the palanquin. Relatives, both men and women, surround her and travel. Here, Sekkizhar Peruman sings about Ammaiyaar as "the unique Thirumagal residing on a lotus seat." He sings about her as Thirumagal (Lakshmi) residing on a lotus flower. However, in this context, referring to Ammaiyaar as "the unique Thirumagal residing on a lotus" should not be interpreted as her being like Saraswati residing on a white lotus or Lakshmi residing on a red lotus. Ammaiyaar should be understood as Gnanaambikai (Goddess of Wisdom) residing on the thousand-petaled lotus, known as Sahasrara Padmam. We can recall here Thayumanavar's verse, "Nārani, the mistress of the mind, beyond qualities, the ultimate power, residing on the thousand-petaled flower." Thus, Ammaiyaar, seated in the palanquin, travels for many days and reaches the Pandya country, the land of beautiful Tamil. Karaikal is a town in a part of the Chola country. The distance between it and the Pandya country is very great. Therefore, they travel for days and reach the outskirts of the town where Paramadattan lives. Paramadattan hears that they have reached the outskirts of the town. He feels great fear in his heart. He is afraid. Just as he was afraid when the mango, which Ammaiyaar had brought by divine grace, disappeared from his hand, he is afraid now. Therefore, he takes his second wife and his child and comes to the place where Ammaiyaar is. Before Punithavathi Ammaiyaar enters his house, he decides that he must go and worship Ammaiyaar. So he comes with his wife and child to the place where Ammaiyaar is. This was the time when Paramadattan's daughter had started to walk. Therefore, these three people arrive at the place where Ammaiyaar is. As soon as they arrive, Paramadattan, his second wife, and their daughter all fall at Ammaiyaar's feet and worship her. As soon as he arrives, Paramadattan quickly falls at Ammaiyaar's feet and worships her. This is because Ammaiyaar has been separated from her husband for a long time. Therefore, they are now meeting after such a long separation. So, as a wife, before Ammaiyaar falls at Paramadattan's feet, he quickly falls at Ammaiyaar's feet and worships her. This is because, for Paramadattan, he sees and worships Ammaiyaar as a divine woman. However, the relatives are unaware of all these matters. Therefore, Paramadattan

Paramadattan bows at Ammaiyaar's feet and says, "I am living by your grace. I have named this daughter of mine after you," and prostrates himself many times on the ground, worshipping Ammaiyaar. By thus worshipping, he made everyone realize that he worships Ammaiyaar as a deity. Ammaiyaar and her relatives are bewildered, not knowing why he is worshipping in this manner. Ammaiyaar, seeing him fall at her feet and worship her, joins her relatives, standing aside where they are. Sekkizhar Peruman sings, "Seeing her husband worship, the beautiful creeper-like woman, along with everyone, stood aside with fear among the relatives." Here, Sekkizhar Peruman refers to Ammaiyaar as a "creeper-like woman." A creeper needs something to cling to and grow. Until now, Ammaiyaar had been clinging to her husband as her support. Now, with her husband falling at her feet, she cannot cling to that support and instead clings to the relatives nearby as her support. Sekkizhar Peruman uses the simile of Ammaiyaar being like a creeper to convey this. Ammaiyaar is now standing aside with her relatives, with fear. The relatives all ask Paramadattan, "Why are you falling at your wife's feet and worshipping her?" Paramadattan begins to answer. We will continue to reflect. Thiruchitrambalam.
