Chapter 07 — Amar-Niti Nayanar

Thiruchitrambalam. We are contemplating the Puranam of Amar-Niti Nayanar in the Thillai Vazh Andanar Sarga of Periya Puranam. Lord Shiva, who came as a Brahmachari Brahmin, gave one of the two loincloths he had on his staff to Amar-Niti Nayanar, saying that he would bathe in the Kaveri and that Amar-Niti Nayanar should keep it safe until then. He added that if it rained and the loincloth in his hand got wet, this one might be needed, so he should keep it carefully. Amar-Niti Nayanar accepted the loincloth and bid him farewell, saying, "Please return soon after your bath." Lord Shiva, in the form of the Brahmin Brahmachari, departed from there to bathe in the Kaveri. The Lord is omnipresent. Therefore, "departed from there" should be understood as the omnipresent Lord, who appeared as a Brahmin Brahmachari, made that appearance disappear. Amar-Niti Nayanar, a great devotee who received the loincloth from the Lord, kept it very carefully in a separate, special place, not with the other old loincloths he kept for other devotees, bearing in mind the words of Lord Shiva, the foremost among Brahmins. In this verse, Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to Lord Shiva as "Mundai Andanar." "Mundai Andanar" means the one who existed before all Brahmins, the first one. If we keep tracing back, father, father's father, his father, and so on, the one who stands first at the end of this lineage is our Lord Shiva. Furthermore, sages like Kashyapa originated from Brahma, the Brahmin, and through them, creation came into being in the world. Therefore, Brahma, the Brahmin, is called Pitamahan (grandfather). His father is Vishnu. And His father is Lord Shiva. Thus, it is also indicated that Lord Shiva is prior to all Brahmins. Moreover, the Upanishads state that our Lord Shiva is the Brahmin who created Brahma, the primordial Brahmin, and taught him the Vedas. Amar-Niti Nayanar kept the loincloth given by the Brahmin Brahmachari very safely. But what did this Vedic scholar, who went to bathe, do? He made the loincloth that Amar-Niti Nayanar had kept safe disappear from there. He made it vanish. The Lord made this loincloth disappear. What is the inner meaning, the philosophical explanation, of making it disappear? Saiva Siddhanta explains the concept of Maya through the doctrine of Satkaryavada. What is Satkaryavada? It means that what exists does not perish, and what does not exist does not come into being. This is the summary of Satkaryavada. If so, here the Lord made an existing object, the loincloth, disappear. It is said that He made it cease to exist. But according to Saiva Siddhanta scriptures and Satkaryavada, an existing object does not perish. So how did this loincloth disappear? The philosophy behind this is that during creation, the Lord takes a part from Maya, the primordial substance, and creates the universes. Then, during dissolution (samhara), He absorbs these universes. Where does He absorb them? Into Maya. He creates this universe from Maya and then, during dissolution, absorbs it back into Maya. Therefore, Maya is the cause for this world. This world, the universe, is the effect. The truth is that the effect merges into its cause. Effects are always formless. Effects are always with form. Therefore, this world, which is visible and perceivable in form,

when it merges into its cause, Maya, during the period of dissolution (samhara), it transforms into a formless entity. That is, it disappears in its causal form, which is a truth of Saiva Siddhanta scriptures. Therefore, the disappearance or vanishing of the loincloth here does not mean that the existing loincloth was destroyed. We should understand it to mean that the Lord made the loincloth disappear into its cause, in such a way that no one could see it. Sivakavimani blesses us with this explanation. Similarly, in every line, every word, and every phrase of Periya Puranam, the truths of Saiva Siddhanta scriptures are embedded. Now, let's continue the story. The Vedic scholar who went to bathe in the Kaveri thus made the loincloth disappear. It is not known whether this Vedic scholar, who went to bathe, returned after bathing in the Kaveri or after bathing in the Ganga. But Sekkizhar Swamigal states that he returned drenched as rain, water from the sky, poured down. The Vedic scholar who went to bathe in the Kaveri returns. As he returns, he causes rain to fall. The Vedic scholar comes, drenched in that rain. Therefore, those in the monastery and near the monastery saw the Vedic scholar coming, drenched in the rain. But Sekkizhar Swamigal sets the scene here, singing that they would not know whether this Vedic scholar bathed in the Kaveri or the Ganga. However, the Vedic scholar who came was a man of truth. Therefore, he would do as he said. So, having said he was going to bathe in the Kaveri and would return, he must have definitely bathed in the Kaveri. But Sekkizhar writes here that everyone thought they did not know whether he bathed in the Kaveri or the Ganga. Lord Shiva, who came as the Vedic scholar, concealed his matted locks and came with his hair tied up. Therefore, he also concealed the Ganga that was in his matted locks. So, it is unlikely that he bathed in the Ganga. But the Vedic scholar is coming, drenched in rain from the sky, making one doubt whether he bathed in the Kaveri. Whether it is the Kaveri or the Ganga, the clouds that formed as water in the sky poured down as rain, and that rainwater from the sky becomes both the Ganga water and the Kaveri water. Therefore, it is also a hint that by being drenched in this rain, he is considered to have bathed in these rivers. As the Vedic scholar came drenched in the rain, our devotee Amar-Niti Nayanar, filled with mature love, before the Vedic scholar arrived, had already meticulously prepared the six-taste divine ambrosia according to culinary rules and waited. When the Vedic scholar arrived, he went to receive him and stood bowing. The Brahmin Brahmachari, who had bathed in the sacred Kaveri or Ganga, and then was drenched in the sky-water, the rain, thought that all this water was impure and its purity was insufficient. Desiring to bathe in the superior purity of the devotee's love, he now proceeds to do the following, says Sekkizhar Swamigal here. The Lord desires to bathe in the pure water of love. That is, it is pleasing to the Lord to see love manifest in devotees, to be delighted by it, to accept that love, and to bestow grace. The Lord is one who has no desires or dislikes. Even so, the only thing He desires from His devotees is love. Thus, the Lord greatly desires to bathe in the pure water of the devotees' love. When does water become pure water? When does ordinary water become sacred water? It becomes sacred and holy water only by touching the body of the Lord,

that is, by touching the divine form of the Lord. All other waters that do not touch the divine form of the Lord do not bestow purity. All other waters are merely physical water. But the water that touches and flows from the divine form of the Lord is sacred water. Bearing this in mind, if we consider it, the Brahmin Brahmachari was drenched in rainwater that poured from the sky. Therefore, it was impure water. Since he was drenched in such impure water, to atone for it, the Lord comes towards the monastery to bathe in the water of Amar-Niti Nayanar's pure love. During temple festivals, when the Lord's processional deities are taken out in processions through the streets, if they happen to get wet in the rain, according to the Agamas, it is considered impure. So, a Punyaham (purification ritual) is performed, followed by an Abhishekam (ablution) to purify them. This is an Agamic rule. Therefore, just as when the Lord is paraded in the streets during a festival and gets wet in the rain, a Punyaham is performed and an Abhishekam with sacred water is done, here too, Lord Shiva, who came as a Brahmin Brahmachari, got drenched in the rain. To perform a Punyaham for this, He needs to receive an Abhishekam with sacred water. Therefore, the Lord came towards Amar-Niti Nayanar's monastery to receive an Abhishekam, considering Amar-Niti Nayanar's pure love as the sacred water. Sekkizhar describes who this Lord was who came. Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to that Lord as "Kovanam Kalvar" (the thief of the loincloth). In the Vedas, Sri Rudram addresses the Lord as "O thief, salutations to you. O chief of thieves, salutations to you." "My heart-stealing thief" is the divine utterance of Thirugnanasambandar Peruman. Why does Sekkizhar refer to the Lord as "Kovanam Kalvar" here? Here, the loincloth belongs to the Lord Himself. That is, it belongs to the Brahmin Brahmachari. But he entrusts it to Amar-Niti Nayanar. He gives it to him. Thus, the Lord makes the item given to Amar-Niti Nayanar disappear from there without his knowledge, thereby causing Amar-Niti Nayanar to be at fault. Therefore, Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to the Lord as "Kovanam Kalvar." That is, Sekkizhar Swamigal says that He is the one who committed a deceptive act through the loincloth. This "Kovanam Kalvar" tells Amar-Niti Nayanar, "I have come completely drenched in the rain. Therefore, the loincloth on the staff in my hand has become wet. So, please bring the loincloth I already gave you." The Brahmin Brahmachari asks Amar-Niti Nayanar for that loincloth. Amar-Niti Nayanar, unaware that the loincloth had disappeared due to the deceit of the visiting Ayya, goes inside and searches for it. The loincloth is not found where he had placed it. Seeing that the loincloth he had carefully kept had vanished, he is bewildered and searches for it. In this verse, Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to Lord Shiva, who came as a Brahmin Brahmachari and made the loincloth disappear, as "Kaithavam Udaiya Ayya" (the deceitful Ayya). "Kaithavam" means falsehood, deceit. Though an Ayya is a person of greatness, worthy of respect, this visiting Ayya, with falsehood and deceit, made the loincloth disappear. "Kaithavam," meaning falsehood and deceit, is not a characteristic of a respectable Ayya.

"I will not speak deceit" is from Thirugnanasambandar Puranam. But this respectable Ayya makes the loincloth disappear from where it was kept. He makes it disappear without Amar-Niti Nayanar knowing. Then he makes Amar-Niti Nayanar search for the loincloth and makes Amar-Niti Nayanar say that the loincloth is not there, thus performing many actions. All these are qualities called "kaithavam," meaning falsehood or deceit. That is why Sekkizhar Swamigal here uses the figure of speech "muranaṇi" (oxymoron) to refer to Lord Shiva, who came as the Brahmin Brahmachari, as "Kaithavam Udaiya Ayya." What is its inner meaning? The Lord's deceit, this "kaithavam" of falsehood, here signifies His power of concealment. Souls know only if the Lord makes them know. If they do not know by His grace, souls cannot know. Appar Swamigal said this: "Who can see if You do not show? Who can see, O Lord with the eye on Your forehead, if You do not show?" Therefore, here, "the deceitful Ayya" is used by Sekkizhar Swamigal to refer to the Lord's power of concealment, His Tirodhana Shakti, as the inner meaning. Let us continue to contemplate. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 08 — Amar-Niti Nayanar

Thiruchitrambalam. We are contemplating the Puranam of Amar-Niti Nayanar in the Thillai Vazh Andanar Sarga of Periya Puranam. Amar-Niti Nayanar is bewildered upon finding that the loincloth is missing from the place where he had kept it. Amar-Niti Nayanar's mind is pure, devoid of impurities like envy, desire, and anger. Therefore, Amar-Niti Nayanar is certain about the place where he kept the loincloth. It was only a short while ago that he placed it there. He is bewildered to find it missing from that spot. Usually, when a lost item is searched for, we search with doubt, wondering if we placed it here or there. This doubt and forgetfulness arise in us due to the impurities in our minds, such as envy, desire, and anger. But Amar-Niti Nayanar had no such doubt. He was certain that this was the place where he kept the loincloth. Yet, the loincloth was not there. That is why he is bewildered. Why did Amar-Niti Nayanar not doubt, as we would, whether he placed it here or there? Because there was not even a trace of impurity in his mind. Therefore, Amar-Niti Nayanar doubts, thinking, "The loincloth has disappeared from the place where I kept it; did it disappear by itself?" So, he searches among all the other clothes he had. The loincloth is not found anywhere. Amar-Niti Nayanar fell into great distress, caught in the trick played by the visiting Vedic scholar. Amar-Niti Nayanar feels great sorrow, thinking that such an incident has befallen him, his wife, and his relatives. He thinks, "Such a misfortune has come upon us," and grieves. The item he received from the Vedic scholar has gone missing. Therefore, he must give a proper answer to the Vedic scholar. Moreover, Amar-Niti Nayanar is distressed in two ways: that the item is lost, and that trouble has befallen the Vedic scholar because of him. Sekkizhar notes here that this distress was not something Amar-Niti Nayanar brought upon himself. It came to him beyond his attention and care, surpassing his situation. When our distress becomes excessive, our actions momentarily freeze. We stop. But Amar-Niti Nayanar, even when such great distress came upon him, did not freeze. He immediately takes action. He grieves, thinking that the visiting devotee would suffer from the cold, drenched in rain, with no way to change his wet loincloth. Without the slightest delay, he quickly sets off, taking another loincloth for the Brahmin to change into, to replace the wet one. This swift action shows his unending love and affection for the devotees of Shiva. Thus, taking a new loincloth from inside his house, he comes out of the house and reaches the Vedic scholar. Sivakavimani Ayya explains that his coming out from inside his dwelling, that is, from his home, implicitly signifies Amar-Niti Nayanar transcending all worldly attachments like his house and possessions, which he considered his own, and moving away from them, thereby aligning himself and all his possessions and worldly attachments with Shiva. Thus, coming out of the house, he approaches the Brahmin Brahmachari, the Lord, and says,

"I had kept the loincloth you gave me safely. But it is not found there now. It has disappeared. No one else has taken it or hidden it. I do not know if it disappeared by itself or what happened. I don't know. I have never seen such a wonder anywhere else," he says. "Therefore, I have brought another good loincloth. It is not a loincloth torn from another cloth. It is a separate loincloth woven specifically as a loincloth. I have brought it with the desire that you should change your wet one and wear this. O devotee with a forehead adorned with sacred ash, please remove your wet loincloth and wear this dry, new loincloth in its place, and forgive my fault," Amar-Niti Nayanar pleads with the Brahmin Brahmachari. The loincloth Amar-Niti Nayanar offered was not as good as the one given by the Brahmin Brahmachari. However, this loincloth was better than all the other loincloths Amar-Niti Nayanar possessed. Therefore, Amar-Niti Nayanar requests him to change his wet loincloth and wear this dry one. Furthermore, Amar-Niti Nayanar requests him to forgive his fault. Here, Amar-Niti Nayanar has not committed any fault. Yet, he admits to having committed a fault and asks for forgiveness. Here, Amar-Niti Nayanar has not committed any fault. But why does he admit to having committed a fault and seek forgiveness? Faults are of two types: intentional (buddhipurvam) and unintentional (abuddhipurvam). An intentional fault is one done knowingly, deliberately, and with planning. An unintentional fault is one committed unknowingly. However, these categories of intentional and unintentional faults depend on the person who committed the fault. These two divisions exist from the perspective of the one who committed the fault. But whether a fault is committed intentionally or unintentionally, the suffering that comes to those affected by the fault is the same, whether the fault was intentional or unintentional; it causes suffering. There is no difference in the suffering that comes to the affected. That is why Amar-Niti Nayanar says that this action of his, though it happened unknowingly, is still a fault. Furthermore, due to that fault committed unknowingly, a devotee of Shiva, an ascetic, is affected. Amar-Niti Nayanar considers why this fault, committed against an ascetic, a devotee of Shiva, becomes a great fault. Therefore, he says, "my small great fault," meaning he admits that his small action is a great fault, and tells the Brahmachari, but also states that the fault was not committed knowingly by him. Saying this, he bows. Bowing here shows Amar-Niti Nayanar bending his body, stooping, pleading, prostrating, humbling himself, and praising the ascetic. The greatness and superiority of the loincloth given by the devotee of Shiva were diminished by the smallness of its disappearance. That is why Amar-Niti Nayanar bows before the Brahmin Brahmachari. Now, the Brahmin Brahmachari speaks to Amar-Niti Nayanar: "Amar-Niti, what you say is good. It hasn't been long since I gave you the loincloth. I gave it to you just now, today. But you keep that loincloth yourself and say you are giving another loincloth in its place. You have been spreading news in the world for many days that you give loincloths to all Shiva devotees. Was the act you did only to usurp my loincloth? Your business is doing well," saying this, the Brahmin Brahmachari, the Lord who had come, became angry, with sparks flying from his eyes. Thus, Lord Shiva, who came as a Brahmin Brahmachari, becomes angry. Lord Shiva, who had concealed the deer in his hand and instead carried a staff, became angry in this manner. Amar-Niti Nayanar's senses were all bewildered, his face withered, and he fell to the ground, exhausted, at the feet of the Brahmin Brahmachari, bowing and saying, "Please forgive this great fault of mine, a small person. This is not a fault that happened knowingly to this servant." Here, Sekkizhar Swamigal, describing Lord Shiva, says that Lord Shiva concealed the deer he held in his hand and came as a Brahmachari carrying a staff. Lord Shiva holds a deer in his divine hands. But here, when he comes in the guise of a Brahmachari, he carries a staff in his hands, befitting the Brahmachari's state. Why does the Lord hold a deer in his divine hands? A deer arose from the yagna performed by the Rishis of Darukavana, intending to destroy this world. The Lord held that deer, which was sent by the Rishis of Darukavana, on the tips of his two fingers. This story is recounted in various Puranas. Here, two states of the Lord are mentioned. One is the state of holding the deer in his divine hands, and the other is the state of coming as a Brahmachari carrying a staff. The first form, holding the deer, is a principle called "showing a deer to catch a deer." When the Lord comes to take souls into His grace, He comes as a Guru in human form. Saiva Siddhanta explains this as "showing a deer to catch a deer." Now, the Lord has come as a Brahmachari carrying a staff. In a classroom, a teacher wakes up sleeping students with the staff or stick in his hand. "Like the act of waking a sleeping student before the teacher with a powerful staff," says Thirumandiram. Sivakavimani Ayya explains these subtleties. Now, the anger (vegutchi) that the Lord assumed is the form of His primordial power, Tirodhana Shakti. This later becomes Arul Shakti (power of grace). Furthermore, the fact that the Lord, who came as a Brahmachari, became angry should be understood as "He appeared to be angry." In reality, the Lord was not angry here. He only appeared to be angry. If the Lord were truly angry, this world would be destroyed. The destruction of Manmatha by the Lord's angry gaze, and the destruction of Brahma and the Tripuras, are well-known stories. Seeing the Brahmachari who pretended to be angry, Amar-Niti Nayanar's senses were all bewildered, his face withered, and he bowed, saying, "This is a fault that happened unknowingly to me. Please forgive this. Instead of this loincloth, I will give you good silk garments and jewels. Please accept them, O Lord." With a disturbed mind and great fear, Amar-Niti Nayanar bowed many times at the feet of the Brahmin Brahmachari. Let us continue to contemplate. Thiruchitrambalam.

Chapter 09 — Amar-Niti Nayanar

Thiruchitrambalam. We are contemplating the Puranam of Amar-Niti Nayanar in the Thillai Vazh Andanar Sarga of Periya Puranam. Amar-Niti Nayanar pleads with the Brahmin Brahmachari, who had come, offering gold, jewels, silk garments, and gems in place of the lost loincloth, and prostrates many times. Hearing this, the Brahmin Brahmachari, feigning a slightly calmed mind, says, "What use are the gold garments, gems, and other things you offer to me? What am I going to do with them? It is enough if you give me a loincloth that is equal in value to the loincloth I wear." For someone in the Brahmachari state, these jewels, gold garments, and gems are of no use. That is why the Brahmin Brahmachari says, "All that you offer will be of no use to me. It is enough if you give me a loincloth equal to mine." Hearing this divine instruction, Amar-Niti Nayanar's mind brightens. Until now, his senses were all bewildered and confused, filled with great fear, and his body trembled as he stood before the Brahmin Brahmachari, Amar-Niti Nayanar, who was like a lion among merchants. Why did Amar-Niti Nayanar, who was like a lion in business, stand trembling and shaking before the Brahmin Brahmachari? It was solely due to the thought that he should not commit any offense against Shiva's devotees, solely due to that fear. This shows Amar-Niti Nayanar's unending love and devotion towards the Lord's devotees. When the Brahmin Brahmachari said that a loincloth equal to his own would suffice, Amar-Niti Nayanar, hearing this, asked the Brahmin Brahmachari, "Yes, I will give you a loincloth equal to yours. But I need your loincloth to place on the scale." Then, the Brahmin Brahmachari gave Amar-Niti Nayanar the wet loincloth, drenched by rain, tied to the tip of the staff he held in his hand, and said, "I gave you one earlier, didn't I? That loincloth and this loincloth are equal. So, give me a loincloth equal to this one." The Brahmin Brahmachari had three loincloths. He was wearing one of them around his waist. So, that loincloth could not be placed on the scale. Apart from that, he had two other loincloths. He had them tied to his staff. Before setting off to bathe in the Kaveri, he gave one of those two loincloths to Amar-Niti Nayanar. The other one is now wet from the rain. The loincloth given to Amar-Niti Nayanar has disappeared. So, the Brahmin Brahmachari asks Amar-Niti Nayanar to give him a loincloth equal to the one remaining loincloth. When this Brahmin Brahmachari first came, the two loincloths tied to the staff he held in his hand were of equal weight. If two loincloths are woven separately, they might not be equal to each other; there could be differences in weight. There could also be differences in size. But if two loincloths are torn equally from the same cloth, they will be equal. The loincloths tied to the staff carried by the Brahmin Brahmachari were such loincloths, torn into two from the same cloth and woven. That is why he says that this loincloth, wet from the rain, is equal to the lost loincloth, and asks for a loincloth equal to it. Furthermore, this Brahmin Brahmachari, without desiring any other object, says that he only wants a loincloth equal to his own.

He takes it. Hearing what the Brahmin Brahmachari said, Amar-Niti Nayanar brought a balance and set it up there. Amar-Niti Nayanar was a merchant, so he always kept a balance for buying and selling goods. Buying and selling was his profession. "For those who thought 'we will see in this trick,' He removed even that trick," says Thiruvachagam. Its meaning is that no matter what tricks or strategies anyone employs to escape, it is the nature of the Lord to enter into those very tricks, transcend their cunning, and bestow grace. This is the meaning of this line from Thiruvachagam. Here, Amar-Niti Nayanar was a merchant. So, in accordance with the practice of trade, by giving one item and asking for an equal item through the balance, the Lord blessed Amar-Niti Nayanar through his trade and took him into His grace. We can relate this to the above lines from Thiruvachagam and rejoice. In one pan of the balance brought by Amar-Niti Nayanar, the Brahmin Brahmachari places his loincloth. Amar-Niti Nayanar places the new loincloth he brought in the other pan. But the two pans are not equal. The pan with the loincloth brought by the Brahmin Brahmachari was lower. The pan with the loincloth placed by Amar-Niti Nayanar rose up. The two pans were not equal. That is, the loincloth placed by Amar-Niti Nayanar was not equal to the weight of the loincloth given by the Brahmin Brahmachari. So now, Amar-Niti Nayanar brings all the loincloths he had kept to give to the Lord's devotees and places them one by one in his pan. Even after placing all the loincloths, the pan on Amar-Niti Nayanar's side remained higher. The pan with the Brahmin Brahmachari's loincloth remained lower. All the loincloths placed by Amar-Niti Nayanar were not equal to that weight. Amar-Niti Nayanar witnesses this miracle. A miracle is a feeling of awe in a state of not knowing what it is. This is an illusion not found in the world. Even though countless loincloths are placed for this one loincloth, they are not equal, he marvels. Amar-Niti Nayanar continued to place fine cloths, silks, and other items, but his pan remained elevated. Earlier, Amar-Niti Nayanar experienced a feeling of wonder. Now he stands amazed. Wonder is a feeling of awe in a state of not knowing what it is. But amazement is a feeling of awe that arises from understanding the reason for something. Earlier, Amar-Niti Nayanar was amazed to see that all the loincloths were not equal to the Brahmachari's loincloth. Now, realizing and determining that the reason for this is Maya, he stands amazed. Therefore, now, in addition to loincloths, he places more silks, cloths, and gold garments in that pan. The Brahmachari asked only for a loincloth. Only a loincloth. He did not ask for jewels, gems, gold, or garments. That is why Amar-Niti Nayanar first placed only loincloths. But even after placing all the loincloths in that pan, the loincloths ran out. Still, the pan did not balance. Therefore, after the loincloths, he places various silk garments in that pan. He places them in the pan on Amar-Niti Nayanar's side. He places silk cloths one by one. Still, they do not balance. So, he places bundles and bundles of silk cloths in Amar-Niti Nayanar's pans. Still, the two pans do not balance. What happens next?

Chapter 10 — Amar-Niti Nayanar

Thiruchitrambalam. We are contemplating the Puranam of Amar-Niti Nayanar in the Thillai Vazh Andanar Sarga of Periya Puranam. Amar-Niti Nayanar first places the loincloth he held in his hand to match the loincloth of Lord Shiva, who came as a Brahmin Brahmachari. The two do not balance. Then he places all the loincloths he had kept to distribute to all devotees. Even then, the balance does not stand even. Then he places all the fabric items he possessed there. Even then, it does not balance. At this point, Sekkizhar Swamigal has composed a wonderful verse to express the greatness of Amar-Niti Nayanar's love and the greatness of Lord Shiva's grace. In this instance, silk clothes and all precious garments are placed in Amar-Niti Nayanar's pan. But that pan remains elevated. In the other pan, that is, the Brahmin Brahmachari's pan, there is only his single loincloth. But that pan remains lowered. To convey this, Sekkizhar Swamigal illustrates the principle that "when the pan containing the boundless love of devotees is considered, the pan containing only the Lord will descend with grace." Sekkizhar Swamigal first states this principle, this truth, and then sings in a way that implies this truth is exemplified by the event taking place in Amar-Niti Nayanar's monastery. It is Sekkizhar Peruman's tradition to first state a principle, a custom, or a truth, and then illustrate it with a story that connects to it. This tradition can be found only in Sekkizhar and Kambar. What is the truth, principle, or justice that Sekkizhar states? It is the truth that the grace of Lord Shiva will yield to the love of devotees, to the love that devotees place upon Lord Shiva. Devotees place love upon Lord Shiva. Lord Shiva bestows grace upon devotees. This is Shiva Dharma. That is, love means what beings place upon the Lord. Grace means what the Lord bestows upon beings. This is the definition of love and grace according to Saiva Siddhanta. This is the characteristic or definition of love and grace according to Shiva Dharma. However, the definitions or characteristics of love and grace according to ordinary worldly dharma and worldly ethical texts are different. That is why Saiva Siddhanta states that worldly dharma, worldly ethical texts, worldly justice, or animal justice are the first step, and Shiva dharma and Shiva merit are the second step. According to worldly justice, according to Dharma Shastra, what is love? What is grace? We can see this through Thirukkural, the Dharma Shastra written in Tamil. In Thirukkural, in the section on Aram (virtue), in Illaraviyal (domestic life), Thiruvalluva Nayanar, who composed the chapters in order, places Ilvazhkai (domestic life), Vazhkai Thunai Nalam (excellence of spouse), Pudhalvarai Perudhal (obtaining children), and Anbudaimai (possession of love). In this chapter on Anbudaimai, he describes the greatness, explanation, and definition of love. Parimelazhagar has written an introductory commentary for this chapter on Anbudaimai, explaining what Anbudaimai means. In it, Parimelazhagar states that love is the affection one has for one's spouse, children, and other related individuals. In short, love is the affection we have for those related to us, or a kind of tenderness that arises in our hearts for those connected to us. Parimelazhagar states that this love is the foundation of domestic life. Now, what is the definition or characteristic of grace in Thirukkural, the worldly Dharma Shastra? In Thirukkural, in the section on Aram, in Thuravaraviyal (ascetic life), the first chapter is Aruludaimai (possession of grace). For this chapter on Aruludaimai, Parimelazhagar states in his introductory commentary: What is grace? It is the natural compassion that extends to all beings, even those with whom one has no connection. Love is the foundation for domestic life, and grace is the foundation for ascetic life. Therefore, according to worldly Dharma Shastra, according to Thirukkural, the worldly Dharma Shastra, love is the affection that arises for those who are related. Grace is the love that naturally arises even for those who are not related. This is the explanation given by great scholars like Thiruvalluvar and Parimelazhagar, who commented on it, for love and grace according to Dharma Shastra. But according to Saiva Siddhanta, according to Shiva Dharma, we have already seen what the great Saiva saints said about love and grace. Here, love is what beings place upon Lord Shiva. Grace is what Lord Shiva bestows upon beings. Based on this, here, what Amar-Niti Nayanar placed upon Lord Shiva is love. What Lord Shiva does for Amar-Niti Nayanar is grace. The sign of Amar-Niti Nayanar's love for Lord Shiva and Shiva's devotees is the loincloths, silk garments, and clothes he placed in the pan on his side. But what Lord Shiva, who came in the form of a Brahmin Brahmachari, placed in his pan is his loincloth, which represents grace. Now, if we look at the truth, the principle, that Sekkizhar states, based on this, the pan representing Amar-Niti Nayanar's love for Lord Shiva and devotees stands elevated. The pan of Lord Shiva, the foremost, the balance pan, stands lowered with grace. Here, in the balance pan, there is one pan for the devotee and one pan for the Lord, the foremost. Love is placed in the devotee's pan. Grace is placed in the Lord's pan. Love is the possession of devotees, the possession of lovers. Grace is the possession of the Lord, the foremost. The pan containing the love placed by devotees stands elevated. The pan containing the grace placed by the Lord stands lowered. Here, grace stands lower than love. That is, the Lord supports the devotees, who are endowed with love, by bestowing His grace. This is the nature of the Lord. Saiva Siddhanta states that it is the nature of the Lord to support devotees who love Him by bestowing grace. "My duty is to serve and remain; His duty is to sustain even me," is the divine utterance of Appar Swamigal. Grace is that which sustains. Love is that which is sustained by grace. It is natural for the sustaining object to be below the sustained object. Whatever sustains will be below. Whatever is sustained will be above. That is why the pan of the Lord's grace is below. The pan of love, placed by devotees and sustained by that grace, stands elevated. Towards those who possess love, that is, towards those who love the Lord,

it is the nature of the Lord to humbly bestow grace and compassion upon those devotees. In Madurai, for the devotee Banabhadran, the Lord came in the guise of a slave and carried firewood, as recounted in Thiruvilaiyadal Puranam. For the love Banabhadran had for the Lord, to bestow grace upon Banabhadran, the Lord carried firewood in the streets of Madurai as Banabhadran's slave. In the same Madurai, for the love of Vandiyamai and for the love of Thiruvadavoor Adigal, Manikkavasagar Swamigal, when the Lord came to bestow grace, He came as a coolie carrying earth and carried earth for a rice cake. The Lord, yielding to the love of Appar Swamigal, came to bestow grace and gave him a bundle of food. For Sundaramoorthy Swamigal, the Lord begged for food and gave it to Sundaramoorthy Swamigal. To Aludaiya Pillai, Thirugnanasambandar Peruman, He gave golden cymbals, a palanquin, and a pearl canopy. He accepted the stone thrown by Sakkiya Nayanar as a flower and bestowed grace. All these divine plays illustrate the nature of the Lord's grace, which yields to love. The Lord's grace sinks into the love of devotees. Grace sinks into love. As the Lord's grace falls upon devotees in this way, their love for the Lord increases more and more. Therefore, it is a tradition we observe that grace sinks into love, and as grace sinks, love overflows and increases more and more. Amar-Niti Nayanar here, with overflowing love, keeps piling more and more clothes into his pan. Here, according to the rule that as love overflows, grace settles, the Lord's loincloth pan settled and went down. "Down" has one meaning as "to be below." It also means "to sustain." Here, the loincloth, that is, the loincloth placed in the Lord's pan, is a symbol of grace. Anything that comes from the Lord is grace, is the embodiment of grace. Here, since the loincloth was used as an instrument for the Lord to take Amar-Niti Nayanar into His grace, that loincloth is the embodiment of grace. It is a custom that grace descends and love ascends. Due to the love that beings place upon the Lord, the Lord bestows grace upon beings. "Grace is the child born of love," says Thirukkural. That is, Thiruvalluva Nayanar says that grace is the child of love. Here, it also fits that beings place love upon the Lord, and as a result, the Lord bestows grace upon beings, says Vidwan Kanchipuram Sabapathy Mudaliar in his commentary. It is the established principle, stated in the Vedas and other texts, that Paramasivan is easily accessible to devotees. Therefore, Kanchipuram Sabapathy Mudaliar also writes in his commentary that Sekkizhar says the loincloth pan descended as if to imply that just like Paramasivan, His loincloth also possesses an accessible nature. Therefore, the pan of the Lord, the foremost, stands lowered with grace to the pan where Amar-Niti Nayanar places his love. The countless loincloths and silk garments placed by Amar-Niti Nayanar in his pan are symbols of love. The loincloth placed by the Brahmin Brahmachari is a symbol of the Lord's grace. Now, "to descend" also has the meaning "to desire." Therefore, as Amar-Niti Nayanar, with love, kept placing more and more silk cloths, and as his love grew, it is also said here that the Lord became desirous of Amar-Niti Nayanar accordingly. The pan of the foremost, which is grace, stands lowered here, doesn't it? Now Sivakavimani explains the next meaning for this. On one side, the devotee's love is placed in the devotee's pan. On the other side,

in the pan of the foremost, there is grace. There is the Lord's grace. Devotees are those who, in their truly loving hearts, shine with the Lord whom they love. Therefore, devotees, having both the devotee and the Lord within them, are two entities: the devotee and the Lord. We have already seen that Thiruvalluvar says love is the mother that gives birth to grace, haven't we? Therefore, here, in the devotee's pan, with both the devotee and the Lord in his mind, meaning both love and grace, while in the Brahmin Brahmachari's pan, there is only the Lord's grace. In the devotee's pan, there is both love and grace. In the Lord's pan, there is only grace. Therefore, according to the principle that two outweighs one, Amar-Niti Nayanar's pan rises and the Lord's pan descends. Thus, this special verse, as one contemplates it, illuminates various philosophical meanings. Sekkizhar Peruman has beautifully placed and sung it here. That verse is the 536th verse, the 35th verse in Amar-Niti Nayanar's Puranam. Devotees with interest can research it further and enjoy it. The verse is: "Muttil anbar tam anbu idum tattukku mudalvar mattu nindra tattu arulodum talvuru vazhakal pattodum tugil aneka kodigal idum pattar tattu merpada talndadu kovana tattu." Chapter 11 — Amar-Niti Nayanar

Thiruchitrambalam. We are contemplating the Puranam of Amar-Niti Nayanar in the Thillai Vazh Andanar Sarga of Periya Puranam. While the pan containing the Brahmin Brahmachari's loincloth remains low, Amar-Niti Nayanar's pan, with various cloths and silk garments, rises high. Amazed by this, Amar-Niti Nayanar approaches the Brahmin Brahmachari and pleads, "I have placed all my clothes, all my fabric items, here. Yet, this pan does not balance. Therefore, please grant me permission to place my other possessions." Hearing this, Lord Shiva, the consort of the Goddess, assents and says, "What else is there for me to say? Place your other possessions, your other items. Any item will do. I just need an item equal to my loincloth. That is all I ask." In this verse, Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to the Lord as "Mangai Pakar" (the one who has the Goddess as a part). In all the verses until now, Sekkizhar Swamigal referred to the Lord as a Brahmin Brahmachari, a Vedic Brahmachari. Here, for the first time, Sekkizhar Swamigal refers to the Lord as "Mangai Pakar." Its meaning is "the one who has Uma Devi as one part." This is because the Lord, who came, is about to bestow grace upon Amar-Niti Nayanar very soon. It is the tradition we have seen that when the Lord bestows grace upon beings, He should come with Uma Devi. Therefore, Sekkizhar Swamigal subtly sings here, for the first time, referring to the Lord as "Mangai Pakar," in accordance with that tradition. Immediately, Amar-Niti Nayanar placed all his gold, silver, other metals, and all his precious gems, jewels, and other valuables in his pan. Even now, the two pans did not balance. Amar-Niti Nayanar's pan remained elevated. Seeing this, all the people of the world were astonished. At this point, Sekkizhar Peruman says: The Lord's loincloth is the Vedas. This loincloth is made of the essence of the four Vedas, filled with penance, and composed of scriptures. Lord Shiva Himself lovingly places that loincloth on the scale. Will not only Amar-Niti Nayanar's wealth on this small earth, but even all the worlds, be equal to this loincloth? Even saying that all the worlds are not equal to this loincloth would not be a praise for that loincloth. The glory of this loincloth stands many crores of times greater than that, says the divine Sekkizhar Peruman. The threads in Lord Shiva's loincloth are the scriptures of the Vedas. They are also the other artistic texts that explain the Vedas. What is in the loincloth is thread. The Vedas revealed by the Lord are also texts. What is a text? "Just as a thread straightens crookedness, so too a text of merit straightens the crookedness of human minds," says the Nannool sutra. What does this mean? A carpenter, who cuts trees from the forest and brings them to make doors, windows, frames, etc., in houses, always carries a thread with him. Wooden items need to be made from the trees cut and brought from the forest. The trees cut and brought from the forest will be crooked. Therefore, to remove their crookedness and make them into beautiful wooden articles, the carpenter uses the thread he has to straighten the crookedness of the wood. This is the use of a thread or string. Does "nool" (thread) not also mean "book"? Books are those that remove the crookedness in human minds and straighten them.

That is why books are also called "nool" in Tamil. The thread-like string held by a carpenter removes the crookedness of wood. The Vedas and other texts revealed by Lord Shiva, the Vishwakarma of this universe, remove the crookedness in human minds, that is, remove faults, and regulate human minds. That is why Sekkizhar Peruman here uses a pun (siledai) to sing, implying both meanings: that the threads in the Lord's loincloth are the Vedas and the texts that explain the Vedas. Will the small wealth of Amar-Niti Nayanar on this small earth be equal to such a glorious loincloth? No, it will not. Not only that, this earth is a tiny particle of this universe. Is it just the Prithvi (earth) sphere that encompasses this small earth? Even if all the spheres in all the other worlds, such as water and air, are combined, they would be a mere speck when compared to the glory of the Lord's loincloth. Here, Sekkizhar Swamigal mentions various worlds, doesn't he? Worlds are countless abodes provided by the Lord for countless souls of various types to experience their enjoyments. These are called worlds. Each world contains numerous universes. Each universe contains numerous worlds. Saiva Siddhanta states that there are a total of 224 worlds. It is said in Kandapuranam that Surapadman, who tormented all the Devas, ruled 1008 universes. When Lord Muruga, who had a gracious gaze upon him, showed his Vishwaroopam (cosmic form) to Surapadman, saying, "Behold my Vishwaroopam," the 1008 great universes ruled by Surapadman were seen hanging from the tip of a single hair on Lord Muruga's divine foot. Therefore, considering this, we can understand the import of why Sekkizhar Peruman says that all the worlds are not equal to even a tiny speck of the glory of the Lord's loincloth. If we delve deeper into this, according to Saiva Siddhanta scriptures, Maya is of two types: Suddha Maya (pure Maya) and Asuddha Maya (impure Maya). From this Maya, the Lord creates 36 Tattvas (principles). Within these 36 Tattvas, 224 worlds are situated. However, the Lord's loincloth, the Vedas, is the first Tattva among these 36 Tattvas, known as Nada Tattva. All the worlds are contained below this. That is why Sekkizhar Swamigal explains that all the worlds, which are below the Nada Tattva (the Vedas, which are the Lord's loincloth), cannot be equal to it. Even after Amar-Niti Nayanar placed all his wealth in his pan, the two pans did not balance. Seeing this, Amar-Niti Nayanar went before the Brahmin Brahmachari and pleaded, "My Lord, I have placed all our wealth, without leaving anything, in our pan. Now, only I, my wife, and my young son remain. If you agree, please grant us permission to also climb into this pan." The wealth placed by Amar-Niti Nayanar was wealth he had earned himself. It was faultless wealth, sinless wealth. Until now, Amar-Niti Nayanar had thought that this wealth would make the loincloth balance. But even after placing all that wealth, the two pans did not balance. According to Saiva Siddhanta

scriptures, many things are described as manifestations or effects of the Anava Malam (ego impurity). Among them, the most important are the ego ("I") and possessiveness ("mine"). Thinking "I" is ego (ahankaram). Thinking "mine" is possessiveness (mamakaram). Here, Sekkizhar Swamigal implicitly indicates that after Amar-Niti Nayanar placed all the wealth he had earned in his pan, his possessiveness, his sense of "mine," that all those were his wealth, his possessions, was eradicated. Following this, Amar-Niti Nayanar pleads with the Brahmin Brahmachari, saying, "Now only I, my wife, and my child remain. If you agree, we too wish to climb into this pan," doesn't he? Therefore, Sekkizhar Peruman implicitly indicates here that now even the ego of "I" is removed. In this sequence, Amar-Niti Nayanar first mentions his wife, and then his child. A child comes through the wife. Therefore, Sekkizhar Swamigal places the wife first and the child second in the sequence. Now, the wife is a life partner. The wife is a support for life, a life-support. That is why Amar-Niti Nayanar says "my wife." But when referring to his child, he only says "young son." This is because a child is a support for both. Moreover, a child, that is, a child of young age, depends on the mother. That is why he places the child after the wife. Thus, Amar-Niti Nayanar tells the Brahmachari, "I, my wife, and child can climb into this pan for this loincloth, for the pan with this loincloth to stand straight, to balance. If you think we have the qualification for it, if you consent, we three will climb in." Here, Amar-Niti Nayanar says this, realizing the greatness of the loincloth and his own insignificance. The Brahmin Brahmachari, who had until now feigned anger, now, hearing Amar-Niti Nayanar say this, casts his beautiful glance of grace upon Amar-Niti Nayanar. He looks with a glance of grace. This means that the actions of the Lord, which had until now manifested as the power of concealment (Tirodhana Shakti), now manifest as acts of grace. Thus, Lord Shiva, casting a glance of grace upon Amar-Niti Nayanar, resolved to free Amar-Niti Nayanar from this "salakku" (false claim). What is "salakku"? "Salakku" means a false case. The opposite of "valakku" (case) is "salakku." If one applies to claim ownership of one's own property, it is called a "valakku." If one creates a false case, files a false case, claiming ownership of property that does not belong to them, that they have no right to, it is called a "salakku." What is the "salakku" that Amar-Niti Nayanar had? All this world, all these universes, all these worlds, are the Lord's property, the Lord's possession. Yet, people in this world think that these worldly things, which belong only to the Lord, are theirs, things they have no right to. This possessiveness, thinking "my property, my wealth" about Shiva's property that does not belong to them, and the ego of "I," is the true "salakku," the true suffering, that we, as souls, have. Here, in this story, the state of being involved in unreal things is referred to as "salakku." Therefore, Lord Shiva bestows grace here to free Amar-Niti Nayanar from this "salakku." Just as someone stuck in mud and unable to get out is helped by someone on the bank reaching out a hand and pulling them out of that suffering, here Lord Shiva resolved, with great compassion, to free Amar-Niti Nayanar from that "salakku" of being involved in his possessions. Let us continue to contemplate. Thiruchitrambalam.
