Saiva Siddhanta never asks you to choose between action and contemplation; it orders them. **Charya** (service/dasa marga), **Kriya** (devotional worship/satputra marga), **Yoga** (union/saha marga), and **Jnana** (wisdom/sanmarga) are called the **Four Padas** — “feet” or “quarters” — of the *Saiva Agamas*. They are sequential training, not personality quiz: every beginner starts at Charya, every *jnani* once swept a floor.

The genius for beginners is that progress is observable: your relationship to a temple changes. At Charya you clean it; at Kriya you adorn it; at Yoga you see it inside; at Jnana you realize you are its servant and its indweller knows you.

### 1. Why Four Paths?

**Agamic source:** Each *Agama* (28 primary) is divided into four sections. Tamil Siddhanta condenses this into sadhana: *charya* purifies *maya* by using body for others, *kriya* purifies *karma* by offering action, *yoga* steadies the mind to glimpse soul, *jnana* dissolves *anava* by knowing. The sequence mirrors how malas loosen: coarsest first (matter), finest last (egoity).

**Temperament within sequence:** Though sequential, padas accommodate gunas. The restless serve best at first; the aesthetic worship; the introspective meditate; the inquiring study. But no one skips to Jnana, because *anava* without taming mimics Jnana as spiritual bypass — eloquent map, untraveled territory.

**Social history:** This ladder democratized liberation. Jain monasteries prized study; Siddhanta said start by carrying water. That is why Pallava towns under Appar’s influence saw temples become service guilds (*uzhavara padai*) before they became philosophical schools.

### 2. The Four Padas Explained

**1. Charya — Dasa Marga (Way of the Servant):** Outer service without initiation prerequisite. Sweep temple (*uzhavara thondu*), gather flowers, light lamps, feed devotees, speak truth, avoid harm. Deity is Lord, soul is servant. Fruit: *salokam* — dwelling in Shiva’s world (proximity), and most concretely, a mind that no longer despises mundane work. Appar is patron saint: “I swept because I could not argue.” Rules are simple: do for temple what you do for home, but with *Shivarpanam* intention. No Sanskrit needed; gesture suffices. Modern equivalent: volunteer monthly at any shrine, including your kitchen shrine — consistency over intensity.

**2. Kriya — Satputra Marga (Way of the Good Son):** Initiated worship (*diksha* required in strict reading), but beginners can practice *bhavana*-kriya: learn *puja* structure — *abhishekam, alankaram, archanai, naivedyam*. Deity is Father/Mother, soul is child. Key disciplines: daily *atmartha puja* (personal linga/puja), *mantra* (*Namachivaya* with *anga-nyasa*), use of *Agama* manuals (e.g., *Karana Agama*). Fruit: *samipam* — nearness to Shiva. Psychology shifts from “I do” to “We do” — worship is relational, not transactional. Sambandar images this: child with father, demanding, trusting, being corrected. Aesthetic training: learn one *pann* mode; beauty becomes discipline.

**3. Yoga — Saha Marga (Way of the Companion/Friend):** Inner worship after external form is stable. Breath restraint (*pranayama*), withdrawal (*pratyahara*), concentration (*dharana*), meditation (*dhyana*) on Shiva as light in *dahara* (heart lotus), *ajna*, and *sahasrara*. Deity is Friend, soul companion. Tirumular’s *Tirumandiram* (Tantra 3) is textbook here — eight-limbed yoga reoriented to Shiva. Fruit: *sarupam* — likeness to Shiva’s form (subtle body illuminated). Danger: siddhis (powers) appear; Tirumular warns to treat them as roadside flowers, not garlands. Modern beginner: 12 minutes breath-focused meditation on *Namachivaya* after 10 minutes kriya, not instead of it. Yoga without seva and puja inflates *anava*.

**4. Jnana — Sanmarga (Way of Truth):** Understanding *Pati-Pasu-Pasa* via hearing (*keLvi/sravana*), reflection (*sintanai/manana*), Clarification of doubts (*thelivu/nididhyasana*), and abiding (*nishtha*) under a Guru. Deity is Shiva as self’s indweller, soul as Knower whose knowing is not its own. Texts: *Sivajnana Bodham, Sivajnana Siddhiyar, Unmai Vilakkam* studied with commentary (*Mapadiyam*). Fruit: *sayujyam* — inseparable union (not merger). Here *Anava* alone is destroyed; soul sees as Shiva sees, yet remains soul. Patron saint: Manikkavacakar after Tirupperunturai — knowledge as being-taken, not taken.

- Each pada contains the others fractally: there is Charya-inside-Kriya (preparing puja vessels) and Jnana-inside-Charya (knowing why you sweep).

- Progression is not calendar but competence: when service is natural, worship becomes sweet; when worship is steady, mind settles for yoga; when mind is clear, doctrine ceases to be debate.

> “The four padas are not steps of a ladder you kick away; they are four walls of one house — serve, praise, behold, know — and you must live in all four.” — Agamic priest manual gloss.

### 3. Walking the Four Today

**A one-year beginner circuit (urban-realistic):**

*Months 1–3 (Charya):* Choose one fixed service: weekly temple cleaning (or if remote, cleaning a local park/temple stream) + daily five-minute lamp and water offering at home. Keep a *thondu diary*: what did my hands learn today? No reading beyond Periya Puranam stories.

*Months 4–6 (Kriya):* Receive or self-dedicate a simple *atmartha puja*: Shiva linga or picture, 16-step simplified puja crib sheet, *Namachivaya* 108 times with vibhuti. Learn one Sambandar *pann* (*Nattapadai*) to sing during *alankara*. Fruit check: do you look forward to puja, or endure it? If endure, stay in Kriya, add beauty.

*Months 7–9 (Yoga):* Add seated practice: 4-4-4 breath (inhale-hold-exhale) x12 cycles, then *japa* mentally, then 5 minutes silence focusing on “I am not the seer alone.” Use Tirumular verses 540–590 as guide. If restlessness spikes, return to service single day.

*Months 10–12 (Jnana):* Weekly study circle: read *Unmai Vilakkam* verse 1 with commentary, discuss not debate. Practice *arul* reflection: “By what light do I know this knowing?” The answer is lived, not recited.

Each pada’s fruit is verifiable: Charya yields humility that does not perform humility; Kriya yields love of form; Yoga yields steadiness; Jnana yields freedom from needing to be seen as wise. If fruit absent, pada not yet mature — stay, not strive.

For manual detail, see [shaivam.org — Sadhana guide for Four Padas](https://shaivam.org/saiva-siddhanta/charya-kriya-yoga-jnana-sadhana-guide) and [WisdomLib — Jnana Marga](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/jnana-marga) with Tirumandiram etext at [Project Madurai — Tirumandiram](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pm0144.html).

4. Pitfalls and How Each Pada Corrects the Others
Sequence prevents pathologies: **Charya without Kriya** becomes burnout activism — hands serve but heart dries. Kritya corrects by giving hands a face to serve, restoring joy. **Kriya without Yoga** becomes performative ritualism — beautiful but restless; Yoga corrects by internalizing flame so puja does not require audience. **Yoga without Charya** becomes detached calm that despises dishes and deadlines; returning to sweeping re-grounds. **Jnana without all three** becomes articulate emptiness — able to refute every school, unable to weep at Tiruvasagam. That is why Mapadiyam insists the *jnani* still does charya: not for merit, but because union without service forgets the rope that was loosened.

Modern trap is “pick your path” quiz marketing that sells identity: “I am a Jnana person, so I don’t need puja.” Siddhanta calls that *anava* polishing its resume. Instead, ask weekly: “Which pada is weakest this month?” Strengthen that one. Union is house with four walls; painting one wall does not make house.

Historical note: Chidambaram’s four *velis* (streets) around Nataraja shrine are named for four padas; circumambulation literally walks the sequence. If you ever walk them, note how outer streets are widest (charya for everyone) and innermost narrow (jnana for few, quietly).

Siddhanta expects doubt as part of method, not failure.
