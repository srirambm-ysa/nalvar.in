**Periya Puranam** — literally the “Great Purana” — is not mythology in the distant sense. Written in the 12th century by the Chola minister-poet **Sekkizhar**, it is the 12th and final *Tirumurai*, gathering the lives of the 63 *Nayanmars* and the 9 *Thogai Adiyars* introduced by Sundarar’s *Tiruthondathogai*. For a beginner, it answers a question no philosophy text can: what does love of Shiva look like when lived all the way through — in a kitchen, on a battlefield, in a potter’s hut?

Sekkizhar composed it at the request of King Anapaya Chola, who had grown absorbed in the *Jeevaka Chintamani*, a Jain epic. The king wanted a Saiva counter-narrative with equal literary power. Sekkizhar went to Thillai Nataraja Temple in Chidambaram, prayed, and heard the divine opening: *“Ulagellam”* — “All the world...” From that cue, 4,286 verses flowed. That origin matters because the text never presents itself as sectarian triumphalism alone; it is a temple-heard correction about where glory truly belongs — in surrender, not cleverness.

Beginners often assume the Nayanmars are ancient and remote. Sekkizhar deliberately de-mythologizes: he names their villages, their occupations, their flaws. This is Tamil hagiography as social history. You meet potters, hunters, farmers, washermen, kings, and courtesans — the entire social lattice of Tamilakam — and the refrain is the same: birth, learning, wealth, even moral perfection do not qualify you; *anbu* (love) does. That egalitarian core is why the Periya Puranam revived Saivism more effectively than any debate.

### 1. What the Periya Puranam Actually Is

Structure helps. The work opens with the history of **Sundarar** — because his *Tiruthondathogai* (the 11-verse catalog of the 63) is Sekkizhar’s source list. It then proceeds Nayanmar by Nayanmar, loosely chronologically but grouped for literary rhythm, ending with Sundarar again and Sekkizhar’s own humility verse.

Word choice is devotional pedagogy. *Tondar* (servant), *adiyar* (slave of the Lord), *anbar* (lover) recur. Sekkizhar distinguishes *karmic* duty from *bhakti*: the former earns, the latter melts. The verses are in *viruttam* meter, meant to be sung in temple courtyards, not shelved. When you read a translation, read aloud — even in English — to feel the cadence that carried these stories before print.

Historically, the Periya Puranam is also our most reliable window onto early medieval Tamil life. Sekkizhar describes irrigation tanks, palmyra markets, temple endowments, and Chola administration with the eye of a minister. Scholars mine it for economic history; devotees mine it for moral clarity. Both uses are faithful. The text wants you to see that holiness happens inside history, not outside it.

For provenance, start with the Tamil original and a careful English rendering. Primary texts ground you when popular retellings embellish:

- Read the vimari—read the verses: Sekkizhar lists exactly what each saint did, not what tradition later added. Stick to his voice first.

- Notice the *tirukadaikappu*-like closings where Sekkizhar ties each story back to service (*thondu*). That refrain is the philosophy.

- Compare Sundarar’s terse 10-line catalog with Sekkizhar’s elaboration. The gap is where Tamil imagination worked for centuries.

### 2. Three Stories That Define Unwavering Devotion

You don’t need all 63 to begin. Carry three and the rest become variations.

**Kannappar (Thinnanar the hunter):** A hunter chieftain who knows no mantra, no Agamic ritual. He finds a forest lingam and offers what he knows: meat, water carried in his mouth, flowers in his hair, shoes on the garland to test its weight. Priests are horrified — until Shiva tests him. The lingam’s eye bleeds; Kannappar gouges out his own eye with his arrow and offers it. When the second eye bleeds, he places his foot where the eye should be so he can aim correctly, ready to remove his remaining eye. Shiva stops him, restores sight, and grants liberation. Beginner lesson: *bhava* (inner disposition) consecrates the offering, not ritual purity. Sekkizhar lingers not on the blood but on Kannappar’s uncalculating love — he acts before he theologizes. Ask yourself: where do you withhold devotion until you feel “qualified”?

**Karaikkal Ammaiyar (Punithavathi):** The only mother among the 63, a merchant’s daughter who becomes a poet after a mango miracle exposes her husband’s incomprehension. She prays not for reunion but transformation: “Give me the form of a *pey* (ghoul), that I may never leave you.” Shiva grants it; she walks to Kailasa on her head, composes *Arpudha Tiruvandhadhi* and *Tiruirattai Manimalai* — the earliest dated Tevaram hymns. Beginner lesson: devotion may cost social belonging. Ammaiyar’s Pey form is not horror but freedom — a body that cannot be married off, domesticated, or returned. Her songs teach how to love without bargaining.

**Appar’s contemporary, Tiruneelakanta Nayanar (the potter):** He lives by one vow after a marital rift: he and his wife will remain celibate, serving Shiva together through hospitality to devotees and offering clay bowls. When a devotee tests him, even his home is not spared — yet he does not break his vow of service. The story recalibrates “great deeds” — constancy in small service (making a bowl, feeding a guest) is unwavering devotion. If Kannappar is abrupt flame, the potter is steady lamp.

> “My heart did not know strategy; it knew only that He was mine.” — the Periya Puranam’s refrain, in many forms, across all 63 lives.

Together they map Sekkizhar’s definition of *anbu*: immediate (Kannappar), costly (Ammaiyar), and durable (the potter). If you meditate on one story a week, you will have a lived ethics by the third month — not theory, but images that correct you when you bargain with God.

### 3. How to Read the Periya Puranam Without Getting Lost

Beginners drown when they try to read front to back. Read liturgically instead.

**Start with Sundarar’s Tiruthondathogai** (Tirumurai 7, padigam 39). Ten verses name all 63 plus the Thogai Adiyars. Memorize its rhythm. Then pick Sekkizhar’s expansions for 6–8 names you remember. This is how Tamil children traditionally entered — name, then story, then verse.

**Use place.** Most Nayanmars are tied to a *padal petra sthalam* (Tevaram temple). Visit or google: Kannappar is Srikalahasti, Karaikkal Ammaiyar is Karaikal, Appar is Tiruvamur. Seeing the temple tank or palm grove anchors the story in geography and makes pilgrimage a reading practice.

**Keep a thondu notebook.** After each story, write: “What did this saint give without calculation? What am I withholding?” Sekkizhar’s aim is not admiration but imitation — specifically, *adiyar thondu* (service to devotees). The colophon verses explicitly promise that hearing with attention yields the fruit of service itself.

**Avoid hagiographic literalism.** When the potter’s house becomes a test or the lingam bleeds, read with Sekkizhar’s eyes: the miracle verifies innocence, not spectacle. The Periya Puranam rarely lingers on magic; it lingers on the moment a soul decides Shiva is non-negotiable.

Finally, chant one Sekkizhar verse a day. Even the opening *Ulagellam* (“All the worlds praise the Lord who...”) reorients a household. Sound carries what analysis cannot, and that is exactly how the Periya Puranam survived — not on palm leaf alone, but on tongues.

4. Sekkizhar’s Literary Art and Why It Matters for Beginners
Sekkizhar was not a cloistered monk; he was *Uttama Chola Pallavarayan*, the Chola prime minister who had read Kamban, studied Sangam anthologies, and administered revenue. That matters because his poetry carries administrative clarity into bhakti. When he describes the Kaveri flooding in *Tirunalaippovar Nayanar* (Nandanar) or the salt pans near Tiruvotriyur, he writes as one who has walked those bunds. For a beginner, this is an invitation to read sacred literature as embodied cartography — not floating myth but rooted place. Pair a verse with Google Maps and the saint becomes neighbor, not legend.

His intertextual move is equally pedagogical. By opening with *“Ulagellam”* — echoing the *Tirukkural’s* invocation of the world — he positions Saivism not as sect but as Tamil civilization’s common ethic. The Chola court expected *kavya* sophistication; Sekkizhar gives it without sacrificing Tamil directness. He never uses a Sanskrit loan when a Tamil word will melt a heart, yet he lets Sanskrit theological terms (*mala, pasa, arul*) enter precisely where doctrine needs precision. That bilingual restraint is why beginners from non-Tamil backgrounds can enter through translation without losing flavor, and Tamil readers feel at home without being insular.

Study tip: read one story in both a prose retelling and Sekkizhar’s verse (even in transliteration). Notice what the verse retains that prose drops — usually the saint’s final prayer line, where the entire biography condenses into a single longing. That line is the mantra of the story. For Kannappar it is *“Kannenna...”*; for Karaikkal Ammaiyar *“Peyuruven...”*. Memorize that line, not the plot, and the story works inside you during traffic or illness.

Finally, address the Jain counterpoint honestly. The court’s fascination with *Jeevaka Chintamani* was not apostasy but aesthetic admiration; Sekkizhar does not demonize Jain monks. He shows Appar leaving Jainism not because it is evil but because its severe asceticism without *aruli* could not heal his sulai. That nuance preserves inter-religious respect while asserting Saiva distinctiveness — a model for modern pluralism where reverence need not become equivalence.
