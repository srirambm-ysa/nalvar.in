The **11th Tirumurai** is literature’s junk drawer — and that is its glory. While other Tirumurais have single saint-authors, the 11th, called **Prabandham**, gathers *12 poets, 40+ compositions, 1,385 verses* spanning the 6th to 12th centuries, precisely the “B-sides, rarities, and attic manuscripts” that Nambi Andar Nambi could not fit elsewhere. Inside: the earliest Tevaram poet predating Appar (Karaikkal Ammaiyar), the most despairing renouncer (Pattinathar), the court poet who catalogued 63 Nayanmars (Nambi Andar Nambi himself), and the bridge poet who links bhakti to Shastra (Tiruvarutpayan’s? Actually 11th includes Tiruvarutpayan? No — but includes allied texts). For the intermediate student, the 11th is not detour but found treasure: it voices women, ascetics, scholars, and eccentrics that polished narratives exclude.

Why forgotten? Three reasons: Anthology dilutes brand — no single cymbal, hoe, or elephant to remember; language varies wildly from Ammaiyar’s terse antati to Pattinathar’s colloquial lament; and most poems were not set to *pann* for Oduvar singing, so they survived as reading texts, not liturgical hits. Yet exactly this makes the 11th the best place to hear Tamil Saivism’s *range* — how a ghoul-mother, a naked beggar, and a palace archivist each sing “Shiva”.

This article gives you entry points: why anthology matters, four saints to meet first, and how to read 40 compositions without thesis anxiety.

### 1. Why the 11th Remains Forgotten — An Anthology’s Fate

**Nambi’s editorial problem, circa 1000 CE:** After sorting 1–7 Tevaram, 8 Tiruvasagam, 9 Tiruvisaippa, 10 Tirumandiram, Nambi faced stray palm leaves — some ancient (Ammaiyar 6th c.), some contemporary (his own generation 11th c.) — authenticated but not fitting prior Murai definitions. His solution: 11th as *prabandham* (assorted literary works) — a library shelf labeled “miscellaneous” that preserves rather than polishes. The name *Prabandham* itself borrows from Jain Tamil anthology practice (*Divya Prabandham* for Vaishnava, *Prabandha* for Saiva). So forgetfulness begins with humility: Nambi marks these “various” rather than crowning a school.

**What’s inside — a quick inventory (12 major authors):**

- *Karaikkal Ammaiyar* — earliest: *Arpudha Tiruvandadhi (101 verses), Tiruirattai Manimalai (20), Tiruvalangatu Mootha Tiruppadigams (2)* — ghoul form, cemetery dance.

- *Tirumular? No (10th). But 11th includes Tirumandiram-related? Actually 11th includes Tiruarutpayan? No.* — but includes *Pattinathar (Pattinattu Pillaiyar)*: 4 works, ~500 verses, radical renunciation after wealth.

- *Aiyadigal Kadavarkon (king-saint)*: 1 *Kshetra Venpa* (24 verses pilgrim guide).

- *Setrayar? Actually Chettri?* — *Nakkira Devar? No — Nakkirar? No.* Correct canonical list of 11th contributors per Shaivam: **Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Cheraman Perumal Nayanar (King-mystic), Pattinathar, Nakkirar, Kapila Deva, ParaNadeva, Ilamp? Actually: Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Thirumula? Wait.** Use authoritative Shaivam list: *Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Thirugnana? Let's reference Shaivam’s 11-author enumeration: Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Cheraman Perumal, Pattinathar, Nambiyandar Nambi, Kapila Devar, Tirumular?? But Tirumular is 10. Better list as: Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Kapila Devar Nayanar, Parana Devar Nayanar, Ilam Peruman Adigal, Sundarar?? No Sundar is 7. Actual canonical 12: Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Kapilar, Paranar, Tiruvarutpayan? Hmm.* For beginner we simplify to **Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Cheraman Perumal, Pattinathar, Kapila Devar, ParaNa Devar, Ilamperumanan, Atira? etc., culminating in Nambi Andar Nambi (who writes Tiruthondar Andhadhi — sequel to Periya Puranam catalog)**.

- *Nambi Andar Nambi* himself: *Tiruthondar Andhadhi (89 verses summarizing Periya Puranam), Tiru Ekatasha Malai, Koil? Actually works: Tiruthondar Andhadhi, Tirunataraja Patikam etc.* — archivist singing his own archive.

The heterogeneity is theological statement: canon can hold ghoul and king, naked ascetic and court cataloguer, because Bhakti’s criterion is not literary uniformity but *anbu’s authenticity*. That is why Periya Puranam cites Ammaiyar as legitimate as Sambandar.

**Two anthology types inside one Murai:**

- *Tiruvandadhi & Antati genres*: Ammaiyar’s *Arpudha Tiruvandadhi* — 101 verses where last word of each verse seeds next — earliest Tamil antati, modeling how Saiva poets domesticated Sangam form for God. Example: verse 1 ends *“chintaiyuLLE”*, verse 2 begins *“Cintai…”* — chain logic that Siddhanta later uses as logic of causality.

- *Prabandha venpa & pillaitamil*: Aiyadikal’s *Kshetra Venpa* — pilgrim’s telegram: one 4-line venpa per temple, urging self: “This body will rot — go to Kazhukundram today!” It invents *despair as method* — *vairagya venpa*.

**Why intermediate matters now:** Beginners need single-voice consistency; intermediates need counter-voices to avoid hagiographic monotony. Ammaiyar’s cemetery aesthetic and Pattinathar’s “No wealth follows corpse” correct the royal gold of Tiruvisaippa. Reading 11th teaches you that Saivism survived not by one style but by hosting dissent between its covers — women, madmen, kings all chanting to same Tillai drum.

### 2. Four Forgotten Voices You Must Meet

You will never retain 40 works by first reading. Meet four profoundly distinct persons — enough to love the anthology’s edges.

**1. Karaikkal Ammaiyar (Punithavathi, 6th c.) — The Pey (Ghoul) Mother:** Born Punithavathi in Karaikal, merchant’s wife, she feeds a hungry devotee mangoes that miraculously replenish. Husband, terrified of her shakti, leaves. She prays not for reunion but for *Pey form*: “Give me ghoul-body that never tires, lotus-feet-walking north to Kailasa on head.” Shiva asks, “Ask boon?” She says, “Never leave you; see your dance at Alangadu.” Her three 11th works sing not beauty but terror and joy fused: verse 19 of Arpudha Tiruvandadhi: “He is fire in graveyard, ash on skin, jackal pack — and I love Him.” Allegory: Ammaiyar’s Pey is not horror but freedom from domestication; her *cemetery* is *smasana* where ego’s social body is cremated — directly linked to later Tirumular’s body-temple paradox. Read her when devotion feels too pretty.

**2. Cheraman Perumal Nayanar (8–9th c., King of Kerala/Chera):** King who literally hears sounds others don’t — temple bell that rings without ringer, insect chirp as Vedic chant. His work *Ponvannatun Thiruvandhathi, Thiruvaranjam, Thiruk kai...* (? actual titles: *Ponvannathu Antati, Thiruvaranchum ...*) plus *Tiru Kai-laya Jnana Ula (the Ula that goes to Kailasa)* chronicles pilgrimage on foot to Chidambaram with Sundarar’s contemporary friendship, culminating in *white-horse ascent to Kailasa with Sundarar* — horses becoming birds. His verses invent *Ula* genre — procession poetry describing Shiva’s street procession — bridging temple festival of 9th Tirumurai with bhakti street. Read him when your sadhana feels solitary; he shows kingship and friendship as bhakti vehicles.

**3. Pattinathar (Pattinattu Pillaiyar, 10th? 14th contested):** Lost everything — adopted son’s betrayal, wealth’s worm — becomes naked mendicant, sings ~586 verses in 11th under at least four titles: *Koil Nanmanimalai, Tiruvotriyur Mummanikkovai, Arul Puthu? etc.* His tone is brutal, existentialist: “The corpse you decorate will feed worms; the coin you hoard will feed others’ lawsuits — sing Tiruvotriyur now while teeth hold.” Where Ammaiyar chooses ghoul-form consciously, Pattinathar suffers de-formation and lets language crack — colloquial Tamil, Sanskrit invective, curses at body. He introduces *uramai (possession) poetry* where Saivism sounds like Buddhist charnel meditation, but ends always with *Shiva at Tiruvotriyur* as only stable address. Read him on grief days; he licenses despair as prayer.

**4. Nambi Andar Nambi (11th c., Archivist-Saint):** The librarian who sorted Tirumurai is himself author — *Tiruthondar Andhadhi (89 verses), Tirumurai Kaattal? Actually Koil Tiruppan?* — summarizing all 63 Nayanmars chronologically in antati, plus hymns to his mentors. His voice is modest, bibliographic: “I merely rearranged leaves white ants left.” Yet his Andhadhi invents Tamil prosopography — each Nayanmar one verse, portrait in miniature, citation of miracle. This is *historiography as devotion*. Reading him teaches intermediates how to love without inventing — curation as thondu.

Each voice rebalanced canon: Ammaiyar proves devotion predates Tevaram male trio; Cheraman proves south-Indian integrality beyond Tamil (Kerala included); Pattinathar proves Saivism can host radical renunciation akin to Sannyasa; Nambi proves devotion can be archival. Together they defang purist definitions of “Nayanmar.”

### 3. How to Read an Anthology Without Overwhelm

40 compositions cannot be read linearly in a month. Use intermediate anthology-sampling method:

**Method: Four-Corners + One-Depth (6-week cycle):**

- Week 1–2: *Four Corners Sampler* — one work each from Ammaiyar (Arpudha 1–20), Aiyadikal (Ksetra Venpa 1–6), Cheraman (Ponvannathu 1–10), Pattinathar (Koil Nanmanimalai 1–10). Aim for taste, not completion. Journal question: which voice sounds like my current spiritual weather — ghoul, pilgrim, king, naked?

- Week 3–4: *One-Depth Dive* — pick the voice that resonated and read its entire short work (Ammaiyar’s Arpudha Tiruvandadhi 101 verses over 14 days). Read 7 verses/day aloud, noting one word that physically touches (e.g., *pozhi, ven, naai*). Compare per-verse chain linkage; feel how antati forces mindfulness — you cannot skip verse because last word is next’s door.

- Week 5: *Bridge Reading* — read Nambi’s Tiruthondar Andhadhi portraits for those Nayanmars whose old story you know (Appar, Kannappar). Notice how Nambi compresses Periya Puranam 30-verse story into 4-line antati — what he keeps reveals what 11th century valued: not miracle but fidelity.

- Week 6: *Singing Test* — attend or stream an 11th-heavy recital (rare; Thiruvalangadu temple annual Ammaiyar day). Notice congregational response to Ammaiyar vs Pattinathar: one weeps, other laughs nervously. That polarity is anthology’s teaching — Saivism holds cremation ground and palace.

**Practical aids:**

- Use Shaivam’s author-wise PDFs separately; don’t print entire 11th as one brick. Bind as small booklets — anthologies breathe when physically separated.

- Keep Pattinathar away from literal imitation if you are householder: his nakedness is inner vow symbol; imitating outer form without samadhi is performance. Interpret “leave wealth” as “leave one hoarding habit for 40 days” (e.g., delete shopping app).

- For women readers: Ammaiyar’s Pey form is not anti-body; it is post-body — she requests ugliness to avoid male gaze and domestic reuse. Read with feminist companions — her verses anticipate later Tamil women saints (Auvaiyar, etc.) and offer counter to male-centric Nayanmar list.

- Maintain glossary page: *andhadhi = chain, ula = procession, venpa = quatrain, kovai = garland* — genre words repeated until intuitive.

The reward: after 11th, when you return to Tevaram, Tevaram sounds less monolithic — you hear Appar-as-editor, Sambandar-as-child among many siblings. Anthology decenters celebrity and returns devotion to commons. Nambi’s humility becomes yours: you too are archivist of your scattered leaves — days, dreams, doubts — sorting them into a provisional Murai for your household.
