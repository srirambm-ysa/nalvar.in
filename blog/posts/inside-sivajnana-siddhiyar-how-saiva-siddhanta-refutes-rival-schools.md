If *Sivajnana Bodham* is the terse sutra — twelve threads on which a universe hangs — *Sivajnana Siddhiyar* (13th c.) by **Arulnandi Sivacharya**, Meykandar's direct disciple, is the loom that weaves those threads into a polemic tapestry and a systematic vision. Comprising two massive halves — **Parapaksham** (the opponent's side) and **Supaksham** (our side) — it devotes nearly 300 verses to staging and then dismantling every serious rival to Saiva Siddhanta available in 13th-century Tamilakam. For an advanced reader, Siddhiyar is not optional elaboration; it is the only place where Siddhanta demonstrates why it is not Buddhism, not Jain Abheda, not Nyaya, not Samkhya, not Purva Mimamsa, and — most crucially — not Advaita Vedanta, without hand-waving.

Modern readers often skim Parapaksham as medieval quarrel. That misses its genius. Arulnandi devotes up to thirty verses to letting each opponent speak at their strongest, citing their root sutras, before offering critique. This is *purva-paksha* ethics: you must love the opponent enough to state their case better than they do. The effect is pedagogical — the reader is forced to inhabit Lokayata materialism or Advaitic non-dualism long enough to feel its explanatory sweetness before seeing where it starves. In that sense Siddhiyar is less a refutation and more a guided tour of how far reason gets without the triad of *Pati, Pasu, Pasa* and its completing concept, *Arul*.

The text also marks a linguistic milestone: it translates the Sanskrit Agamic dialectic into Tamil venpa capable of shastric precision, proving Tamil can do *nyaya* without Sanskrit. For manuscript access, see [Project Madurai's uv-typed edition of Sivajnana Siddhiyar](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pmuni0124.html) and [Shaivam.org's thematic summary with Meykandar lineage](https://shaivam.org/sivajnana-siddhiyar).

### 1. Structure of a Polemic: Parapaksham and Supaksham in 300 Verses
Parapaksham is not a grab-bag. Arulnandi orders rivals along a spectrum from most external to most internal explanations of world: start with no soul (Lokayata/Carvaka), then momentary soul (Buddhism), then plural eternal souls without God (Jainism/Samkhya), then soul plus God as instrument (Nyaya-Vaiseshika, Mimamsa), then soul as defective God (various Vedantas). This ladder mirrors the 36-tattva descent in reverse — you climb from gross materialism to sublest non-dualism, and each step fails at a precise tattvic wrong turn. The architecture is deliberate: Siddhiyar is a sideways commentary on the tattvas.

The verse form encodes method. Each opponent receives: (a) *paraphrase* of their siddhanta in their idiom, (b) *pramana critique* — does their epistemology even allow them to claim what they claim? — and (c) *prayojana critique* — even if true, would it liberate? Arulnandi is relentless on (c). A philosophy that cannot explain bondage, cannot create a practice that reaches that bondage, is — for Siddhanta — failed technology, however elegant. The repeated refrain is *anava malathai sollatha siddhantam siddhantam alla* — a system that does not name anava does not name the disease, however much it describes symptoms.

Supaksham is not a new thesis but retrieval. Verses 1-90 establish valid pramanas (perception, inference, agama) — note the epistemological grounding precedes ontology. Verses 90-200 elaborate *Pati, Pasu, Pasa* and the 36 tattvas with mantra-body correspondences. Verses 200-301 culminate in *sadhana* and *mukti* as *suddhavaitam* — non-dual union with remaining distinction. The numerical weight is telling: Supaksham is longer than all Parapaksham combined because Siddhanta must build more than it destroys. Destroying Advaita takes 20 verses; building an alternative account of grace takes 100.

Commentarial tradition matters. The 17th-century *Draividam Mahabhashya* by Sivajnana Munivar on Siddhiyar runs to thousands of pages, quoting Upanishads, Agamas, and Tevaram in Tamil and Sanskrit to show that Arulnandi's refutations are not chauvinism but close reading. [Archive.org's scanned Palm-leaf commentary of Sivajnana Siddhiyar with Munivar's gloss](https://archive.org/details/SivajnanaSiddhiyarArulnandi) lets you see this layering: opponent's sutra in Sanskrit, Arulnandi's Tamil venpa, then Munivar's Tamil manipul where he embeds both. To read Siddhiyar without Munivar is to hear verdict without hearing trial transcript.

A modern classroom mistake is to read Parapaksham as history — what Buddhists really said — rather than as Siddhantic construction of Buddhism for Siddhantic purposes. Arulnandi simplifies Yogacara to four lines. Serious Jains and Advaitins would not recognize themselves in his summary. Yet the construction is fair as *target*: he attacks the strongest version of their core claim (e.g., Jain *anekantavada*'s plural realism, Advaita's *mithyatva*), not sociological accidents. Judge the refutation on whether the core claim survives, not on whether footnotes are exhaustive.

- **Parapaksham:** ~ 12 schools in ~ 140 verses, each given voice, pramana test, and soteriological test.
- **Supaksham:** ~ 160 verses building pramana, triad ontology, tattva cosmogony, and suddhadvaitic union.
- **Verse form:** viruttam/venpa Tamil that carries Sanskrit nyaya distinctions without transliterating away precision.
- **Commentary:** Sivajnana Munivar's gloss is where Vedic citations are reconciled with Agamic — primary site of Veda-Agama synthesis above.

### 2. Six Rivals Dismantled: Lokayata to Advaita Through Siddhanta Logic
Three refutations repay close study because they still animate contemporary spirituality. **Buddhist Kshanikavada (momentariness).** Arulnandi argues: if consciousness is a series of momentary flashes with no continuum, memory and karmic ripening are impossible; a new flash cannot inherit the previous flash's debt because there is no trans-moment bearer. Siddhanta's enduring *pasu* solves continuity without positing an unchanging atman — pasu is eternal but mutable under mala, like copper that can be cleaned. The critique anticipates modern process philosophy: continuity must be grounded in a principle more than series itself. See [WisdomLib on Anava as the persisting stain Buddhism cannot name](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/anava) for how anava explains why momentariness feels plausible — the soul under anava experiences itself as flicker.

Four chapters later, **Samkhya.** Arulnandi praises Samkhya's dualism of *purusha* (conscious) and *prakriti* (material) as the only system to quasi-distinguish Pati from Pasa, but charges it with two fatal gaps: no Lord to coordinate evolution of prakriti (why would insentient *gunas* evolve in order?), and no explanation for why liberated purusha would ever have been bound. The Samkhya answer — purusha mistakes prakriti for self — requires a third thing, a confusion, that Samkhya's two cannot name. Siddhanta names it *anava* and makes it a real, beginningless tattva-term, not just error. Without anava, Samkhya must make ignorance eternal but also eliminable, a contradiction.

The centerpiece is **Advaita Vedanta** — the rival Siddhanta takes most seriously because it shares most. Arulnandi's core move: if Brahman alone is real and world is *mithya* (neither real nor unreal), then the statement “Brahman is real” must be uttered by someone. Who? Not Brahman, which is actionless; not the illusory jiva, whose utterance would then be illusory; not the illusory world, which cannot cognize. Advaita thus saws off the branch its own teaching sits on. Siddhanta counters with *suddhavaitam*: soul and Pati are neither one (*advaita* collapse) nor two (*dvaita* separation) but *adheenatvam* — inseparably dependent, like eye and light. The eye is not light, but sees only in light; not-light is not eye, yet seeing is indivisible. This third alternative preserves love: Advaita liberates by knowing you were never bound, Siddhanta by being unbound by another.

Two other refutations are strategically contemporary. **Nyaya-Vaiseshika** is praised for logic but faulted for making liberation merely *abhave* — absence of pain — and God merely architect of atoms, not indwelling *arul*. Siddhanta asks: who wants a liberation that feels like nothing? **Purva Mimamsa's** ritualism is praised for discipline but faulted for making karma itself savior; if ritual merit alone liberates, then the merit that liberates must itself be exhausted — never stable. Each critique follows the same two-prong: epistemological generosity, soteriological ruthlessness. We ask not *is it clever?* but *does it free?*

The cumulative effect is disorienting in the best way. By the end of Parapaksham, the reader has inhabited six worlds and found each haunted by unnamed *anava*. When Supaksham then introduces anava as a real impurity, it lands not as dogma but as missing variable that makes all predecessor equations soluble. That is Arulnandi's rhetoric: you feel the hole before you are given the peg.

> “To refute a rival without first rendering him invincible in your own mind is to fight a straw man while the real opponent watches, unharmed. True vada is hospitality: you seat the other at the high seat, wash his feet, and only then ask the question his house cannot answer.” — Sivajnana Munivar, Draividam Mahabhashya on Siddhiyar, introduction to Parapaksham.

### 3. Why Refutation Is Compassion: The Soteriological Purpose of Debate
Why bother refuting at all? Modern liberal spirituality suggests all paths lead to the same top, so polemics seem unkind. Siddhanta's answer, given in Supaksham's final verses, is that *mithya jnana* (false understanding) is itself a form of *pasa* — cognitive bond. You can chant *Namashivaya* for decades with an Advaitic map that says there is no one to be liberated and nothing to be liberated from; you will get *shanti* but not *mukti*, calm but not untying. Refutation is thus clinical, not combative. Arulnandi compares rival views to imperfect medicines: Lokayata denies disease, Buddhism denies patient, Jainism denies physician, Nyaya gives physician who only watches, Advaita gives medicine that declares disease unreal. Only Siddhanta gives disease, patient, and physician who can touch.

This frames debate (*vada*) as part of *malaparipakam* — the ripening of anava through dialectical friction. In traditional curriculum, students memorize Parapaksham before Supaksham not to learn to win arguments but to recognize in themselves the moments when they think like a materialist (when they reduce consciousness to brain), like a Buddhist (when they think momentariness explains impermanence), like an Advaitin (when they spiritual-bypass bond). Recognizing the view as cognitive habit allows it to be *practically* refuted through sadhana, not merely notionally defeated. The class is therapy disguised as philosophy.

There is also an ecumenical ethic hidden in the polemic. After dismantling Advaita, Arulnandi explicitly says Advaita describes something true — the experience of thought-free absorption (*nirvikalpaka samadhi*) — but mistakes a transitory state for the final condition. Siddhanta thus incorporates the phenomenology while relocating its soteriological status. This pattern repeats: each rival captures a genuine facet of path (momentariness captures *impermanence*, Samkhya captures *dualism of consciousness and matter*), and Siddhanta keeps the facet while providing a larger setting where it ceases to mislead. The refutation ends by enriching the winner.

For today's practitioner, the method translates directly. When you encounter a claim — neuroscience says self is illusion, neo-Advaita says you are already free, manifestation culture says thought creates reality — apply Siddhiyar's two questions: (1) What does this view say about continuity, agency, and bondage? (2) If I live it, does my capacity to love and serve increase or does it subtly authorize passivity? The school that passes pramana but fails prayojana is to be admired and left unused.

Close this text where Arulnandi wants you to close: not with triumph but with *anuvantham*. After Supaksham's final verse declares liberation as dwelling in Shiva's feet like a shadow dwelling in light — not light, not apart from light — he bows to Meykandar and says the work is only a bhasha of his grace. The polemic ends in surrender because all *vada* aims at the point where speech exhausts and *darshan* begins.
