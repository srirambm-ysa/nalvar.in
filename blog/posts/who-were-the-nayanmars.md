The word **Nayanmar** means simply “devotee who is led and who leads” — one whom Shiva has claimed and who in turn shows others the way. Between the 6th and 9th centuries CE, across the river plains, coastal ports, and hill shrines of Tamil Nadu, 63 such lives flowered and were remembered as **Nayanmars** — not because they founded a sect, but because their love for Shiva burned through every boundary of birth, learning, gender, and wealth. Their history comes to us through **Sundarar’s Tiruthondathogai** (the 11-verse catalogue of saints in Tirumurai 7) and, most fully, through **Sekkizhar’s Periya Puranam** (12th Tirumurai, 4,286 verses) composed in the 12th century at the request of King Anapaya Chola in Chidambaram’s Tillai Nataraja temple.

For the modern beginner, the Nayanmars can feel distant — names on temple pillars, bronze processions during festivals. This article brings them near. We meet who they were socially and historically, linger with the three masters who also gave Saivism its still-sung music, and ask why these 1,400-year-old biographies still change how Tamil Saivas sweep a temple, sing a padigam, or bear suffering. The sources we draw upon are not late legends only: the *Periya Puranam* itself, palm-leaf manuscripts digitized at [Project Madurai — Periya Puranam etext](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pmuni0030_02.html), and the living commentarial tradition summarized at [Shaivam.org: The 63 Nayanmars](https://shaivam.org/nayanmars-63-nayanmars-history), with cross-cultural context at [WisdomLib on Nayanar](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/nayanar) and early English translations archived at [Archive.org — Periya Puranam translation](https://archive.org/details/periyapuranam00seki).

A householder reading this for the first time need not memorize 63 names. Hold a single lens: *anbu* (love) as sole qualification. Sekkizhar repeats it like a drumbeat — kings and potters, Brahmins and hunters, women householders and courtesans stand on one level because love levels. That egalitarian core, more than miracle, is why the Tamil Bhakti movement could not be contained by monastery or palace and why it still forms the ethical heart of Saiva Siddhanta.

### 1. Who Were the Nayanmars?

The list of 63 is not random sanctity but curated sociology. Sekkizhar, a Chola minister who had read the Jain *Jeevaka Chintamani* his king admired, was asked to give Saivism an equal literary monument. He went to Chidambaram, prayed before Nataraja, heard the divine cue *“Ulagellam”* (“All the world...”), and composed a purana that is also a census. He names villages you can still visit — Thiruvamur, Sirkazhi, Tirunavalur, Kanchipuram, Madurai — occupations you still meet — farmer, potter (*kuyavar*), oil-presser, weaver, toddy-tapper, washerman, hunter — and dilemmas you still face — debt, illness, jealousy, caste pride, grief. This is hagiography as social history, and its honesty is its power.

Traditionally the 63 are grouped with the 9 **Thogai Adiyars** (assemblies of devotees) whom Sundarar also praises, making 72 revered figures, but the 63 are individually storied. Begin with five archetypes that teach the whole spectrum:

- **Kannappar (Thinnanar)** — the hunter at Kalahasti who offered his eyes to stanch Shiva’s bleeding lingam, testing love against ritual pollution. Shiva stopped his hand and gave sight. Source: [Shaivam.org on Kannappar](https://shaivam.org/kannappar-nayanar) and [Periya Puranam — Kannappar section](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pmuni0030_02.html).

- **Karaikkal Ammaiyar (Punithavathi)** — merchant’s wife who became “mother of Karaikkal” yet begged Shiva to turn her into a skeletal *pei* (ghoul) so she could witness his dance at Tiruvalangadu without female beauty distracting her. Earliest Tevaram voice, 6th century, mother of Tamil bhakti poetry.

- **Thiruneelakanta Nayanar** — the potter of Chidambaram who kept a vow of chastity after a jealous quarrel and served devotees’ pots; his house is still pointed to near Tillai.

- **Pusalar** — the poor Brahmin who, unable to build a brick temple, built one daily in his heart, installing Shiva by mental consecration for years. Shiva chose his heart-temple’s consecration day over a king’s granite temple at Kanchipuram.

- **Isaignaniyar and Sadaiyanar** — parents of Sundarar, reminding us householders themselves can be Nayanmars; family is not obstacle to sainthood.

Notice the pattern: not one miracle proves doctrine; each life proves a different *upaya* (means) — eye-offering as *atma-nivedana* (self-surrender), ghoul-form as *virakti* (dispassion), pot-making as *charya* (service), heart-temple as *manasa puja* (mental worship). Later Siddhanta maps these onto *Charya, Kriya, Yoga, Jnana*, but Periya Puranam never sermonizes; it narrates.

Historically, the Nayanmars lived when Tamilakam was urbanizing under Pallavas and Pandyas, when Jain *pallies* and Buddhist *viharas* ran schools, hospitals, and courts, and Saiva temples were often deserted, unroofed, weed-choked. Inscriptions at Thiruvatigai and Sirkazhi confirm Jain dominance in 7th century. The Nayanmars’ padayatra — walking barefoot from shrine to shrine, singing, sweeping, and weeping — was thus public restoration. Appar’s hoe (*uzhavara-padai*) cleared temple courts so that community could re-enter; Sambandar’s golden cymbals recalled children to song. Their timeline — 6th (Karaikkal Ammaiyar), 7th (Appar, Sambandar), late 8th (Sundarar), 9th (Manickavasagar, though counted among Nalvar not 63) — overlaps the very centuries Tamil bhakti revived temple economy. You can trace it on the map still: Paadal Petra Sthalams (276 shrines sung by Tevaram) cluster along Kaveri and Vaigai and are exactly the circuit Nayanmars walked.

A beginner’s misreading is to think 63 is closed and distant. Periya Puranam ends by inviting the reader into the *Thogai Adiyars* — indefinite multitudes of servants who never made list but are praised in same breath. Sekkizhar says explicitly: Sundarar could not count all devotees, so he praised groups — “those who serve” — and I, Sekkizhar, praise them too. The canon thus deliberately leaves door open. Devotion remains qualification, not ordination.

> “My caste, my family, my learning — none availed. Only love that would not let go made me Yours.” — Periya Puranam refrain, echoed in every Nayanmar’s verse

To read further, keep [Shaivam.org’s Periya Puranam overview](https://shaivam.org/periya-puranam) and [the English translation at Archive.org](https://archive.org/details/periyapuranam00seki) open beside Sangam geography. Notice how Sekkizhar always begins with place, then person, then crisis, then song — place grounds you, song lifts you.

### 2. The Three Great Masters of Tevaram

Among the 63, three are set apart not by greater holiness but by liturgical function: **Appar (Thirunavukkarasar), Sambandar, and Sundarar** composed the **Tevaram** — Tirumurai 1–7 — the hymns still sung daily by Oduvars in every Siva temple before rice is cooked. Sundarar himself catalogued the 63 in his *Tiruthondathogai*; Appar and Sambandar lived that catalogue. Their lives, told consecutively in Periya Puranam, are paired theology — age and childhood, endurance and wonder, service and song.

**Appar (c. 580–661 CE, Thiruvamur)** — born Marulneekiyar to a Vellala farming family, orphaned, raised by elder sister Thilagavathiyar. Drawn to Jain logic at Thiruppatirippuliyur, he became Dharmasena, head of the Jain monastery, mastering Jain dialectic until burning *soolai* (gastric agony) no Jain rite could heal drove him back to his sister’s *Thiruneeru* (holy ash) and the chant *“Kootrayina Varu Vilakkagileer”* at Thiruvathigai Veerattanam. His pain dissolved, his Jain past forgave, and an enraged Jain community persuaded Pallava King Mahendravarman I to trial him — lime kiln cooled to lotus pond, poison turned to nectar, bound to stone cast into sea at *Kadalil Kal* where stone floated on *Namachivaya*. Each ordeal, sung as proof, turned the king himself Saiva — realm-level bhakti revival by public wonder. Appar thereafter took hoe as symbol — *Uzhavara-padai*, servant of servants — walking to some 125 of 276 Paadal Petra Sthalams, sweeping weeds and thorns from temple courts before singing 3,066 *Thandagam* verses (Tirumurai 4–6), long flowing Tamil that teaches breath control as devotion. He called himself *Adiyarkku adiyen* (servant of servants) and died at Thirupugalur, 81, singing into Siva’s feet. Listen to *Masil Veenaiyum* and *Kootrayina Varu* to taste his humility. His videos are living liturgy: [Thiruvathigai — Appar’s healing temple (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xHobKBS-Ha8).

**Sambandar (c. 640–656 CE, Sirkazhi)** — born Aludaya Pillai to Sivapada Hridayar, three years old, left on tank bund while father bathed at Thoniappar temple. Crying “Amma! Appa!”, he was fed *Gnana-paal* (milk of wisdom) by Uma herself while Shiva watched on Nandi, and his first verse burst forth — *“Thodudaya Seviyan”* — Vedas on a toddler’s tongue. Siva, fearing his bud-soft palms would bruise on hand-cymbals, sent golden *thalam* etched with Pancha-akshara at Thirukkolakka; pearl palanquin, parasol, and Yazhpanar’s yazh followed. He traveled in palanquin to 220 shrines, sang ash to heal the Pandya at Madurai (fire and water ordeals defeating Jains with Appar at his side), gave breath to the dead merchant’s son at Thirumayilai, and to Pumpavai’s daughter from ash-pot. Even Appar bowed and called the child “father” for purity. At 16, at Achalpuram, garlanded for marriage, he sang *“Kallur Perumanam”* and Jyoti opened in sanctum; bride, kin, guests stepped into light and dissolved into Shiva. Sixteen years, 383 padigams, 4,160 verses — luminous, musical, *pann*-setting that still defines Oduvar curriculum. His child-mind teaches that gnana is not earned, it is given. Experience: [Sirkazhi — Sambandar’s birth shrine (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t8Oy54xY0wE) and his very first hymn [Thodudaya Seviyan — sung today (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=sambandar+thodudaiya+seviyan).

**Sundarar (late 8th century, Tirunavalur)** — born Nambi Aruran to Sadaiyanar and Isaignaniyar (both later listed among Nayanmars) — Kailasa’s Alala Sundaran sent to earth. Fostered by chieftain Narasinga Munaiyar, destined for wedding, interrupted by old Brahmin at mandapa waving palm leaf: “This boy is my slave by his grandfather’s deed.” Court upheld deed; old man led him to Thiruvennainallur and revealed as Shiva: “I claimed you because you are Mine.” Half quarrel, half love, Sundarar sang *“Pitha Piraisudi”* — his first sulk with Friend. So began *sakhyam* (friendship) — Shiva as Thambiran Thozhan who fetched gold for his devotee, filled famine granaries, became messenger to reconcile Sundarar with wives Paravai at Thiruvarur and Sangili at Thiruvotriyur, enduring sharp words as *Vanthondan* (wilful one) because friendship tolerates truth. From him flows *Tiruthondathogai* — the first garland of 63 — and 1,026 verses of Tirumurai 7 where household love and god-love mingle without shame. He traveled with Cheraman Perumal Nayanar, Chera king-saint, and at Thiruvanchikulam rose on white elephant to Kailasa while Cheraman rode horse winged by Namachivaya. Sundarar proves married, indebted, jealous life can be sainthood. Hear: [Thiruvennainallur — Sundarar’s deed temple (YouTube)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ICzKVr55bQA).

Textually, Tevaram’s *padigam* form — 10 verses plus 11th *tirukadaikappu* (signature verse naming poet, place, benefit) — was genius preservative. Ten plus one is singable, memorizable, pilgrim-tallied. The *pann* system (precursor to raga, e.g., Nattapadai, Takkeshi, Piyantha Gandharam) assigned melodic mode to each padigam, preserved by Oduvars, codified in inscriptions at Thanjavur Big Temple (Raja Raja’s endowment of 48 Oduvars, inscription 1011 CE). That music, more than manuscript, saved Tevaram when palm leaves were lost (see next article). Beginner tip: learn three padigams — Sambandar’s *Mandiramavathu Neeru* (ash-medicine), Appar’s *Koorayinin Varuvil* (protection), Sundarar’s *Pitha Piraisudi* (friendship) — and you hold the three masters’ throats in yours. Scholarship at [Shaivam.org on Tevaram Panns](https://shaivam.org/tevaram-pann) and manuscripts at [Project Madurai — Tevaram etext](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pmuni0420.html).

### 3. Why Their Stories Still Matter

Why return to 63 old lives when modern Saiva Siddhanta offers Shastra, yoga, and temple science? Because the Nayanmars answer the one question philosophy cannot by argument: what does surrender look like when lived all the way through a kitchen, battlefield, or office? They teach that the path is not escape but *thondu* (service that stoops).

**Social lesson — devotion democratized.** Periya Puranam insists birth never qualifies; *anbu* does. Thiruneelakanta the potter and Nandanar the Dalit pulayan (whose story pulses at Chidambaram’s gate) stand beside Brahmin Sundarar without apology. This radical egalitarianism explains why Tamil Bhakti could not be reduced to Sanskritic orthodoxy alone; it forced temple doors open, literally. Modern Tamil villages still re-enact these stories during *Guru Puja* festivals (each Nayanmar has a nakshatra day) — not as myth but as social memory: we are still that potter, still that hunter. That is living Siddhanta before Shastra.

**Devotional lesson — many ways to love.** The tradition maps nine modes of bhakti onto Nayanmars: Kannappar’s eye is *atma-nivedana*, Karaikkal Ammaiyar’s ghoul is *virakti*, Pusalar’s mind-temple is *dhyana*, Appar’s hoe is *dasa marga* (servant), Sambandar’s cymbals are *satputra marga* (child), Sundarar’s quarrel is *sakhya* (friend). You are not asked to copy eye-offering; you are asked to find your mode and go deep. Contemporary teachers at Dharmapuram Adheenam still prescribe Nayanmar sadhana: choose one Nayanmar whose temptation mirrors yours, read his Puranam daily for mandala (48 days), and let his response rewire yours. That is psychologically astute spiritual practice.

**Textual lesson — canon as testimony, not treatise.** Without Periya Puranam, Tevaram would be beautiful song without story, Shastra without witness. Sekkizhar gives bhakti biography so that later philosophy (Unmai Vilakkam’s 36 tattvas, Sivajnana Bodham’s 12 sutras) never floats. When Umapati cites “as Kannappar showed” in *Tiruvarutpayan*, he is not decorating argument; he is grounding epistemology in witness. That is why advanced Adheenam curricula still require Periya Puranam before Bodham — testimony before theory.

**Practical entry for today — three steps:**

- **Read one Puranam weekly.** Begin with Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar and the three above; read Sekkizhar’s Tamil if able, otherwise [WisdomLib’s summaries](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/periya-puranam) and [Project Madurai](https://www.projectmadurai.org/pm_etexts/utf8/pmuni0030_02.html) palm-leaf. One story, not five — let it saturate.

- **Sing one padigam daily.** Use [Tevaram Oduvar singing — YouTube playlist](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=tevaram+oduvar) to learn by ear, then add *Mandiramavathu Neeru* as daily ash-prayer. Song carries story into breath.

- **Serve one thondu.** Sweep a temple courtyard, wash vessels after Annadhanam, clean a roadside shrine — Appar’s hoe as modern broom. Document how sweeping changes listening.

The Nayanmars ignited Tamil Bhakti not by founding an institution but by proving love outlives institution. When Jain logic dazzled, Appar wept; when royal power coerced, Sambandar sang; when domesticity tempted retreat, Sundarar befriended God and stayed householder. Their fire caught Chidambaram’s vault (next article), set panns ringing in villages, and still waits, like ash on skin, for anyone willing to be claimed.
