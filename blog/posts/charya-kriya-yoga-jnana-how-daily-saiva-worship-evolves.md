**Charya, Kriya, Yoga, Jnana** — the *Four Padas* (literally “feet” or “quarters”) of every Saiva Agama — are often taught at beginner level as a list: service, devotion, meditation, wisdom. That list is accurate but dangerously static. For the intermediate practitioner, the Four Padas are not four personalities to choose from (“I’m a Jnana person”) but one sequential maturation that happens inside a single lifetime, often inside a single day. You begin by sweeping the temple because you cannot yet sit still; you end by seeing that the sweeper, the worshipped, and the broom are held in one Awareness — and then you still sweep, but differently.

This article moves the Four Padas from Beginner to Intermediate by treating them as *evolutionary worship*. After the introductory histories of Pati-Pasu-Pasa and the early symbolism of vibhuti and Nataraja, you are ready to ask: how does *my* worship actually change as *malas* (impurities) ripen? The Agamas answer with exquisite practicality: each Pada has a relationship (dasamarga — servant, satputramarga — child, sahamarga — friend, sanmarga — knowing), a purity target (Maya, Karma, Anava), and a temple analogue that makes progress tactile.

For many modern seekers, daily worship stalls because it plateaus into repetition: same mantra, same incense, same inner weather. The Pada framework reframes repetition as refinement. Charya done with attention prepares Kriya; Kriya done with longing invokes Yoga; Yoga done with honesty dissolves into Jnana. Without this map, practitioners either romanticize Jnana and bypass the body, or sentimentalize Charya and never inquire. With it, every act — cleaning a shrine, arranging flowers, tracing breath — knows its place on a ladder leaning against the sanctum wall.

### 1. Why Worship Must Evolve — Beyond a Menu of Options

The Beginner lesson taught you *what* the Four Padas are. The Intermediate lesson asks *why order matters*. Each Agama (28 root Saiva Agamas: Kamika through Vatula) divides its content into *Charya Pada* (conduct), *Kriya Pada* (ritual), *Yoga Pada* (meditation), and *Jnana Pada* (knowledge). The order is not editorial; it is ontological — mirroring the three *malas* that bind the soul. Coarsest impurity must be softened first with coarsest tool.

**Charya exhausts Maya-mala’s extroversion** by redirecting body and wealth outward in service. Maya is not “illusion” in the Advaita sense; in Saiva Siddhanta it is the real, insentient substratum that becomes world and body — necessary but entangling when mistaken for self. When you use hands to garland, feet to circumambulate, earnings to feed pilgrims, Maya ceases to be net and becomes instrument. That is why *Uzhavara Thondu* (Appar’s hoe-service) is not humility theatre; it is Maya-physics.

**Kriya exhausts Karma-mala’s transactional habit** by turning action into offering. Beginners offer fruit to get fruit; intermediates offer action itself — *kayena vacha manasa* — to Shiva as the Enjoyer. The Agamic puja with its 16 upacharas (from *avahana* invocation to *visarjana* dismissal) externalizes interior attention: lamp is sight offered, incense is breath offered, naivedya is hunger offered. Done daily, Kriya retrains agency from earning to dedicating, which is how karma-seeds stop being sown.

**Yoga thins Anava-mala’s contraction** by withdrawing senses and seeing the soul (*pasu*) as distinct from body-mind yet still distinct from Pati. This is intermediate raja-yoga as described in Tirumandiram chapters on *asanga yoga* (eight auxiliaries) and *paryaya* methods — not gymnastics but *nirodha* (steadying). Here worship moves from outer icon (*sakala*) to inner icon (*sakala-nishkala*) — visualizing Lingam in heart lotus, breath as *hamsa* mantra, syllables as light.

**Jnana dissolves Anava’s root notion “I am the doer-knower”** by listening (*sravana*), reflecting (*manana*), and being (*nididhyasana*) under a Guru who has realized *suddhadvaita* — non-dual union without merger. At this Pada, worship is not act but recognition: soul’s knowing is enabled by Sakti (Arul) yet is soul’s own knowing, as eye sees by light. Tirumular calls this *“Anbe Sivam”* — when love becomes being.

Three misconceptions to drop now:

- *“Jnana is intellectual, so I can start with books.”* In Agama logic, Jnana without purified body and dedicated action is mimicry — parrot Jnanam that recites while Anava remains tight. The body must be taught to serve before the mind is trusted to know.

- *“Charya is for beginners, so I’ve graduated.”* No Pada graduates out. The jnani still performs Charya — but now as *ataraxy* not currency. Sekkizhar shows even liberated Nayanmars sweeping and feeding.

- *“Progress is linear and you level up.”* Padas spiral. A day may hold all four: morning Charya cleaning altar, midday Kriya puja, evening Yoga breath, night Jnana reading of Sivajnana Bodham verse 9. Evolution is deepening, not leaving behind.

### 2. The Four Padas as Lived Experience, Not Theory

To feel the sequence, attach each Pada to a relationship Burma — how you address Shiva when you are in that Pada — and to a concrete temple behaviour.

**Charya — Dasa Marga (Servant): “You are Lord, I am servant.”** Relationship: master-servant. Emotion: humble willingness. Temple analogue: outer precincts — gopuram, tank, garden, kitchen. Service list from Kamika Agama: sweeping (*sodhana*), smearing cow-dung floor, gathering flowers, carrying water, repairing walls, lighting lamps, feeding devotees. Ethical spine: no reward calculus; use body, not wallet alone. Day-markers: you arrive early, leave late, prefer invisible tasks. Symptom of ripening: you stop counting who noticed, and silence while sweeping becomes pleasant rather than performative. Classical exemplar: Appar sweeping with hoe, Tiruneelakanta Nayanar making pots, Poy Adimai Illadha Pulavar weeding.

**Kriya — Satputra Marga (Child): “You are Parent, I am child.”** Relationship: mother-father to son-daughter. Emotion: tender dependence. Temple analogue: mandapa to sanctum doorway — adornment. Ritual list: performing *nitya puja* (daily), *naimittika* (festival), *kamya* (optional) with Agamic steps — invoking, bathing Lingam, clothing, flower garland, incense, food, praise. You now handle forms, not just floors: learning to tie garlands so each flower faces Deity, to ring bell without startling, to pour abhisheka without waste. The shift from Charya is ownership: you no longer clean someone else’s house; you decorate your parent’s house. Intermediate nuance: Kriya expects mantra accuracy — five *Brahma-mantras* for faces, *Hrdaya* mantras for worship — and an ethical “inner Kriya”: tongue, eyes, hands are offered as implements. Exemplar: Sambandar adorning with song-garlands, Sundarar demanding gold as a child would from a parent.

**Yoga — Saha Marga (Friend/Companion): “You are near, I am with You.”** Relationship: friend-companion. Emotion: intimacy that needs no intermediation. Temple analogue: inner chamber — you as moving shrine. Practice list drawn from Tirumandiram Tantra 3 and Agamic Yoga Pada: *yama-niyama* (restraint), *asana* (still posture before Deity), *pranayama* (so-ham breath 21,600 times/day awareness), *pratyahara* (withdrawing from market noise), *dharana* (placing Lingam at six adharas), *dhyana* (single flow), *samadhi* (cessation of separative effort). You learn to do puja internally: breath is incense, heart-beat is drum, attention is lamp. Tirumular’s *Anbe Sivam* verses (270–300) make Yoga not withdrawal from temple but discovering temple’s blueprint within spine. Ripening sign: outer puja time shortens not from laziness but because inner alignment is quicker — you can evoke sanctum presence by closing eyes. Exemplar: Tirumular in cave, yogic Nayanmars like Tirumula-Devar.

**Jnana — San Marga (Truth Way): “You are All, I know because You enable knowing.”** Relationship: soul born of Jnana, teacher as Sakti. Emotion: lucid love. Temple analogue: sanctum-and-beyond — knowing the knower. Method: under a Satguru, study of 14 Meykandar Shastras in order (Unmai Vilakkam → Tiruvarutpayan → Sivaprakasam → Sivajnana Bodham → Sivajnana Siddhiyar → etc.), but studied as *unlearning anava*, not scholarship. Texts like Sivaprakasam explicitly map *how* Arul operates to make knowing possible: Siva-sense enables soul-sense, like sun enables eye. Practice becomes *vichara* after ritual: after puja, sit and ask — who offered? who witnessed? The answer is not “I” but “Sakti in me, soul through Sakti.” Ripening sign: less need to assert realization; more need to serve realized ones. Exemplar: Manikkavacakar after grace-melting, Arunanti after Bodham, Meykandar himself.

Intermediate diagnostic questions for honest self-placement (use journal weekly, not to label permanently):

- When no one sees, do I still straighten the altar cloth? (Charya honesty)

- Do I offer flowers to be seen offering, or because the stone’s coolness on my palm teaches tenderness? (Kriya motive)

- Can I sit 12 minutes without touching phone after puja, watching breath return to nostrils? (Yoga capacity)

- After reading one Bodham sutra, do I argue it or let it argue me until smallness is felt physically? (Jnana receptivity)

> “The broom, the bell, the breath and the book are one stick with four grips. Shift grip as hand tires, but never throw the stick.” — Tiruvavaduthurai Adheenam oral teaching on Four Padas.

Agamic detail: each Pada has its own *diksha* (initiation) — *samaya, vishesha, nirvana, abhisheka* — and its own proximity grades to Sivaloka (*salokya, samipya, sarupya, sayujya*). These are not heavens but capacities. ### 3. Designing an Intermediate Daily Practice Across the Four Padas

Theory without schedule decays. The intermediate practitioner benefits from a *day mandala* that deliberately touches all four Padas, rather than exclusively meditating or exclusively serving. Below is a householder-adapted template distilled from Nitya Karma sections of Karana and Kamika Agamas, plus Oduvar practice at Dharmapuram Adheenam. Adjust time to reality — even 30 minutes total, apportioned intentionally, outperforms 2 hours of unconscious routine.

**Pre-dawn (Brahma muhurta) — Charya (5–10 min):** Wake, sip water, clean worship space as puja — not before puja. Wipe altar slab, refill lamp oil, fold last night’s cloth, dispose yesterdays flowers to plant base (not trash). Recite Tiruvaiyaru? Actually recite Appar’s *Thiru Angam mapping* verse on body as temple while wiping: “Body is temple...” This links Maya-work to embodiment theology. Do not rush to mantra; let broom teach you that impurity is not shame but dust that returns.

**Morning — Kriya (10–20 min):** Perform abbreviated *nitya puja*: *achamana* (sipping), *vibhuti* wearing with *Panchakshara*, invocation (*Avahana*: visualize Shiva with Sakti descending as light into Lingam or picture), then sequentially — water sip (*arghya*), bath (*abhisheka* with plain water if no Lingam), garment (flower), sandal (*gandha* red sandal dot), incense, lamp, food (fruit or cooked rice), *pradakshina* (circumambulation around mental sanctum), prostration. Use Tiruvisaippa or Tevaram verse appropriate to your sthalam affinity; if you follow Nataraja, chant Tiruvempavai 8; if Arunachala, chant Tiruvarutpayan 2. Close not with boon request but dedication: *Mama karma sivaarpanam* — my action Siva-dedicated. Use [shaivam.org — Nitya Puja steps with transliteration](https://shaivam.org/thirumurai/kriya-pada-nitya-puja-steps-tamil-english) for sequence accuracy.

**Evening — Yoga (10–15 min):** After duties, sit on same cleaned cloth now as *asana*. No new ritual. Follow Tirumular’s *Vasi yoga* simplification: inhale through left (*ida*) 4 counts so-ham, retain 8, exhale through right (*pingala*) 8, retain empty 4. Place attention sequentially: navel (fire), heart (air), throat (space), brow (light). Visualize Lingam at heart lotus with Avudai as Shakti pedestal. When thought arises, do not chase; label “maya-form” and return. Three cycles enough for intermediate; steadiness over duration. Record in notebook single line: “Breath touched ___ today, lost at ___.”

**Weekly — Jnana (30–60 min once/week):** With teacher or trusted commentary, read one verse of Unmai Vilakkam or Sivaprakasam — not a chapter. Read Tamil line aloud, read English gloss, sit 5 minutes asking “How does this explain yesterday’s sweeping?” Write one contrarian question and bring to satsanga. This prevents Jnana from floating. End by singing one verse of Tiruvasagam that weeps the same truth — linking Shastra to sobbing.

Common intermediate pitfalls and antidotes:

- **Burnout from Charya perfectionism:** clean as devotion, not sanitation audit. If body pains, offer pain as Charya — sweeping slower is also service.

- **Show Kriya:** hide elaboration when guests watch; do simplest form with full attention. Agamas warn *damBha puja* (show puja) binds more than no puja.

- **Yoga as escape:** if Yoga makes you disdainful of temple bustle, you have mistaken withdrawal for avoidance. Return and hear bustle as mantra.

- **Jnana as argument winner:** if study makes you correct others, pause study and double Charya hours. Sivajnana Bodham Sutra 10 begins with humility prerequisite for a reason.

Measure maturation not by experiences but by *spontaneous ethics*: you gossip less without vow, you feed before eating without calculation, you can be contradicted without heat. Those are Pasa-looseness signs no aura photograph can fake. Keep the four grips rotating, and the stick — your single worship — will wear smooth in the hand that finally knows it is held.
